# 04 - Perception science as an engineering resource: what audiences cannot detect, will assume, and will misremember

Slice: perception, cognition and the science of magic, treated as an engineering resource: which
perceptual and cognitive effects can be reliably triggered with built props. Written for the
technology-transfer review described in `00-brief.md`. Sections 1 to 6 follow the briefed format;
an appendix restates the findings as numeric tolerances for a builder.

Research note. The shared web-search budget ran out after 21 searches, so most of the later
checking was done by opening primary pages directly. Everything with a URL in section 7 was either
opened or appeared in a search result. Anything written from memory is marked "(unverified)" and
has no URL in the text. Component prices are rough late-2026 estimates from memory, not checked against
supplier pages.

## 1. Summary

- **The most useful output of the science-of-magic literature is a set of tolerances, not a set of
  tricks.** It tells a builder how much weight, lag, seam, colour error, motion and inconsistency an
  audience will absorb. The appendix lists 28 of these with numbers.
- **Forcing is quantified.** A salience-based riffle force reached 98% on 118 people with about 90%
  feeling free (Olson 2015); classic force 54% and visual force 45% (Shalom 2013, N = 31); position
  force 52% for one card of four (Pailhès & Kuhn 2020); the Derren Brown style mental priming force
  17.8% against 1.9% chance (Pailhès & Kuhn 2020). Felt freedom does not track actual freedom in any
  of these studies.
- **A chosen object can be swapped after the choice and about three quarters of people will not
  notice**, and will then explain why they chose the thing they did not choose (choice blindness:
  26% detection for faces, and no more than about a quarter of swaps for jam and tea). This is a "swap"
  primitive that needs no cover, only a moment of disengaged attention.
- **Occluded space is perceived, not merely assumed, to be empty, and visible half-objects are
  perceived as whole** (Ekroll's illusion of absence and amodal volume completion). Tricks built on
  these two effects were almost never solved across three viewings, while misdirection tricks were
  mostly solved. Fabricated shells and narrow occluders are therefore the most repeat-proof
  concealment available.
- **Attention-based concealment is weak on repeat.** A coin moving in plain view for 550 ms was
  missed by 54.5% on first viewing and 17.6% after three preview trials (Barnhart & Goldinger
  2014). Technology should take over exactly the actions that currently rely on misdirection.
- **A false solution keeps working after it has been disproved** (Thomas, Didierjean & Kuhn 2018).
  This is the best experimental support for pairing technology with classical method: give
  the audience a sleight theory and then destroy it, and they do not move on to "electronics"; give
  them a visible inert gadget and then open it, and they do not move on to sleight.
- **Inert technology is readily believed.** Olson's switched-off scanner was accepted as reading
  and inserting thoughts; a "psychologist" framing was believed even when the performer was
  introduced as a magician (Lan et al. 2018). Visible technology is better used as a decoy than as
  a method.
- **Verbal suggestion alone changes what about 40% of witnesses report** (a key "still bending":
  39 to 40% with suggestion against 5 to 16% without; 60% with an agreeing co-witness, 23% with a
  dissenting one). A prop only has to supply a small real change for suggestion to carry the rest.
- **Weight is the best haptic lever.** The Weber fraction for lifted weight is about 5%; the
  size-weight illusion is very large (partial eta squared .98) and survived 40 lifts; felt weight
  and length follow the inertia tensor, which a hidden moving mass can change while a scale reads
  the same.
- **Most textbook illusions fail live.** Motion-induced blindness needs 10 s of steady fixation,
  the rubber and marble hand need minutes and correlate with suggestibility, McGurk varies by
  person and language, infrasound gave a 22% report rate. They are listed under dead ends.
- **Magicians' intuitions are measurably wrong in places.** In Wincza & Kuhn (2025) audience
  participation lowered enjoyment, naming a card felt less free than touching one, and an effect in
  the spectator's hand was rated no more impossible than one elsewhere.

## 2. Catalog

### 0. Map of the field

The modern science of magic starts with Kuhn, Amlani & Rensink, "Towards a science of magic"
(*Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 2008) and Macknik, Martinez-Conde and magician co-authors,
"Attention and awareness in stage magic" (*Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 2008), followed by the
popular book *Sleights of Mind* (2010) (all three from memory, unverified). Rensink & Kuhn's
framework paper sets out four levels: magic techniques as lab methods, magic effects as phenomena,
whole tricks as objects of study, and taxonomies of tricks. Kuhn's *Experiencing the Impossible*
(MIT Press, 2019) is the standard book-length synthesis and Matt Tompkins's *The Spectacle of
Illusion* (2019) the historical one (both from memory, unverified). Kuhn's MAGIC Lab moved from
Goldsmiths to Plymouth. The Science of Magic Association runs the field's conference; the next is
8 to 9 June 2027 at The Magic Circle, London. Main groups: Kuhn and Pailhès (forcing, agency,
misdirection), Olson (forcing, sham machines, placebo), Thomas and Didierjean (false solutions,
reasoning), Ekroll (occlusion and completion), Barnhart (attention and timing), Tompkins (illusory
objects), Wiseman and French (suggestion, memory, belief), Johansson and Hall (choice blindness).
Refs: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01508/full ;
https://scienceofmagicassoc.org/

### A. Controlling choice

**A1. Salience and timing forces.**
Olson, Amlani, Raz & Rensink (2015, *Consciousness and Cognition*) riffled a deck so one card was
visible longer: 98% of 118 people on the street chose it and about nine in ten said the choice was
free. A screen version reached only 30%, so the live social setting does much of the work. Shalom
et al. (2013, *PLoS ONE*) measured the classic force at 54% and a visual force at 45% (N = 31) and
found ratings of "being forced" were the same whether the force had worked or not.
*Accessibility:* skill, not hardware. *Magic relevance:* control of choice. The 98% against 30% gap
says a machine that reproduces only the exposure timing (a flip-book, a rotating display) should
expect the lower number unless a person is driving it.
Refs: https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/02/150209113003.htm ;
https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0058254

**A2. Position force.** With four cards in a row and a casual "push one forward", 52% took the
target position; telling people the decision mattered made the choice more deliberate and weakened
the force (Pailhès & Kuhn 2020, *Psychological Research*). *Magic relevance:* any four-object
layout gets a 52% hit before outs; the remaining 48% is cheap to cover by fabricating three
alternative endings.
Ref: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-020-01350-z (seen in search; full text
redirected to a login).

**A3. Mental priming force.** Pailhès & Kuhn (2020, *PNAS*, N = 90): 17.8% named the three of
diamonds (chance 1.9%), 38.9% a three, 33.3% a diamond; 0 of 23 in a no-prime control; live and
video did not differ; replications with 240 people gave 15.4%. Only 3 of the 16 who named it said
they knew why, although 72% had noticed "some gestures". The authors call it "less reliable than
most other forcing principles". *Magic relevance:* too weak as a method, good as a bonus hit, and
strong evidence that people do not connect noticed cues to their own choice.
Ref: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7395500

**A4. Outcome forces (criss-cross, equivoque).** In the criss-cross force only 2 of 30, then 4 of
90, realised their cut had no effect; misdirection and time delay, which 91 surveyed magicians
rated as the key ingredients, made no difference. Even with the force card visibly numbered on its
back only about 17% caught it (Pailhès & Kuhn 2020, *QJEP*). Equivoque is covered in Pailhès,
Kumari & Kuhn (2021, *JEP: General*); I could not open the numbers.
*Magic relevance:* people substitute a simpler procedure for the one they saw. Procedures with one
more step than a spectator can hold in mind are a design resource, and a mechanism can supply the
extra step.
Refs: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7583451 ;
https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/the-magicians-choice-providing-illusory-choice-and-sense-of-agenc/

**A5. What participation actually does.** Wincza & Kuhn (2025, *Psychology of Aesthetics,
Creativity & the Arts*; 200+ magicians, 130+ lay participants): participation reduced enjoyment and
increased confusion; physically selecting a card felt freer than naming one; in-the-hands location
did not raise impossibility.
Ref: https://www.plymouth.ac.uk/news/study-exposes-magicians-misconceptions-about-why-their-tricks-work

### B. What is not seen

**B1. Inattentional blindness in tricks.** Kuhn & Tatler (2005): 18 of 20 live viewers missed a
cigarette dropped into the lap; a video version fell to 43% blindness. Barnhart & Goldinger (2014,
*Frontiers in Psychology*): a coin sliding 4 degrees of visual angle across the table for 550 ms
was missed by 54.5% (18 of 33) on a single viewing and 17.6% (6 of 34) after three preview trials.
General literature: about 50% miss the gorilla; real-world fight noticed by 72%, 56%, 42% as load
rises; similarity to the attended set matters enormously (94% notice a black cross among attended
black letters, 6% a white one).
*Magic relevance:* a visible mechanical action of about half a second is hidden from roughly half
of first-time viewers and almost nobody on a third viewing. Match a mechanism's appearance to
whatever is being ignored, not to what is being watched.
Refs: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01461/full ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inattentional_blindness

**B2. Change and choice blindness.** Johansson, Hall, Sikström & Olsson (2005, *Science*): only 26%
noticed that the photograph handed to them was the one they had rejected; 84% believed they would
notice. Hall et al. (2010, *Cognition*): at most about a quarter of jam and tea swaps detected at
a supermarket stall. Change blindness: a 67 ms blank is enough to hide a change (Pashler 1988);
half of listeners missed a slow three-semitone pitch change.
*Magic relevance:* the swap can happen after the choice, in the open, during the hand-over. The
original method was a magician's double-card on a black surface, so this is magic returning to
magic with measured rates attached.
Refs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choice_blindness ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Change_blindness ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20637455/

**B3. Illusion of absence and amodal completion (Ekroll group).** A hemispherical shell lifted off
a table looks like a whole ball, monocularly or binocularly, and continues to look whole when the
viewer knows it is a shell (Ekroll, Sayim & Wagemans 2013, *i-Perception*); only 29% solved the
billiard-ball routine after repeated exposure (Faber 2012, cited there). Svalebjørg, Øhrn & Ekroll
(2020, *i-Perception*, N = 75, nine tricks shown three times) found absence and completion tricks
had the lowest solution rates at every viewing while attention and reasoning tricks were solved
"by most", and magic ratings for the perceptual tricks did not fall after they were solved. The
illusion of absence is stronger for narrow occluders. Related, from memory: the "shrunken finger"
study (Ekroll et al. 2016, *Current Biology*) and a levitation built on empty-space perception
(Øhrn et al. 2019, *i-Perception*) (both unverified).
*Accessibility:* thin shells are easy in resin or thermoformed sheet. *Magic relevance:* conceal
and duplicate. This is the strongest experimental case for shells and palming-type concealment
over misdirection.
Refs: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4129385 ;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7339904/ ;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7991007/

**B4. Seeing what did not happen.** Vanishing ball: 63% (68% with gaze following the imaginary
ball, 32% with gaze on the hand) reported the ball leaving the hand (Kuhn & Land 2006); nearly one
third saw it with no real throw beforehand (Kuhn & Rensink 2016, *Cognition*). Phantom vanish: 32%
reported seeing an object that was only mimed, with no verbal suggestion (Tompkins, Woods & Aimola
Davies 2016, *Frontiers in Psychology*).
*Magic relevance:* a mechanism has to produce only the start of an event; expectation completes it
for a third to two thirds of viewers. Useful for productions and vanishes where the object never
has to travel.
Refs: https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/16188 ;
https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00950 ;
https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/magic-trick-fools-us-not-our-eyes

### C. What is remembered and how methods are reasoned about

**C1. Suggestion and memory of the event.** Wiseman & Greening (2005): 40% who heard "it's still
bending" reported a stationary key continued to bend, against 5% without. Wilson & French (2014,
*Frontiers in Psychology*, N = 180): 38.9% against 15.6%; a co-witness who agreed pushed it to 50%
overall (60% with suggestion), a dissenting one held it at 15% (23%); believers 37.5%, disbelievers
17.4%. General memory work: about 30% form partial or complete false memories under repeated
suggestion; the effect grows with delay and is reduced, not removed, by warnings. Rensink & Kuhn
note observers remembering five lemons where three appeared.
Refs: https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/crooked-suggestion ;
https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01289/full ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misinformation_effect ;
https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01508/full

**C2. False solutions.** Thomas & Didierjean (2016, *Cognition*): exposing people to an unlikely
false solution ("I influence you with my gestures") stopped them finding the real, simple one (all
cards identical), even when told to assume their idea was wrong. Thomas, Didierjean & Kuhn (2018,
*QJEP*): a false method (palm to back pocket) kept blocking discovery of the real one (a duplicate)
after the magician had shown the false one to be impossible.
*Magic relevance:* experimental backing for Tamariz's method of false solutions in *The Magic Way*
(book from memory, unverified). A disproved theory occupies the slot.
Refs: https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/illusion-of-depth/2016-thomas.pdf ;
https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/it-is-magic-how-impossible-solutions-prevent-the-discovery-of-obv/

**C3. Impossibility as a constructed contradiction.** Smith, Dignum & Sonenberg (2016, *Frontiers
in Psychology*) model impossibility as an unresolvable conflict between a perception-backed belief
about now and a memory-backed expectation. Practical rule: put the strongest evidence where
scrutiny is highest (start and end) and do the hidden work in the middle, where events are expected
to be ordinary and are poorly remembered.
Ref: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00748/full

**C4. Too-perfect theory.** Rick Johnsson, *Hierophant* 1970, building on Vernon; precedents in
Annemann (1935) and Monk Watson (1945); responses by Tommy Wonder, Jamy Ian Swiss, Ortiz and others
in *Genii* August 2001; Tom Stone argued it fails even as a diagnostic. An empirical test exists
(Pailhès, Lee & Kuhn 2022, *PeerJ*, "Too perfect to be good?"); the page returned 403 and I will
not quote results from memory (unverified).
Refs: https://wiki.geniimagazine.com/index.php?title=Too_Perfect_Theory ;
https://peerj.com/articles/13449v1/decision/attachment (seen in search only).

**C5. Attribution when technology or "psychology" is on offer.** I found no experiment that
directly measures how often lay audiences say "it's electronic"; this is a gap. The nearest
evidence:
- Lan, Mohr, Hu & Kuhn (2018, *PLoS ONE*): after a mind-reading act explained as psychological
  skill, belief in micro-expression reading and priming rose, and it made no difference whether the
  performer was introduced as a magician or a psychologist.
- A 2021 *Acta Psychologica* study (authors not confirmed): people readily explain mental effects
  by psychological skill but not physical ones; analytical thinkers explain mental effects by
  physical props.
- Silvia, Crasson, Greengross & Kuhn (2025): supernatural attributions are rare.
- Olson, Demacheva & Raz: none of 167 children found the secret of a pen vanish, and both the
  youngest and oldest viewers were overconfident in wrong explanations.
- Olson, Landry, Appourchaux & Raz (2016): participants accepted that an inactive MRI scanner read
  and inserted numbers, reported less control and slower decisions under "influence", and described
  "a force" or a number "popping in". A later pilot used a dead scanner as a "placebo machine" with
  children; 90% of parents reported improvement at one month (no sample size given).
*Reading for a builder:* audiences adopt whatever explanation the presentation makes available and
stop there. Magician theory says the same thing: Ortiz's *Designing Miracles* (separate cause from
effect in time, space and concept) and Derren Brown's writing on pseudo-explanations (both from
memory, unverified).
Refs: https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/25085 ;
https://doaj.org/article/f4ce90becc7649948c279dadd560b420 ;
https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/more-than-just-a-magic-trick-exploring-an-audiences-supernatural-/ ;
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25798117/ ;
https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/using-cocktail-magic-and-fmri-psychologists-implanted-thoughts-peoples-minds ;
https://mcgillnews.mcgill.ca/conjuring-up-a-new-approach-to-placebos/

### D. Weight and touch

**D1. Weight discrimination and weight illusions.** Weber fraction for lifted weight is about 5%
(105 g against 100 g). Size-weight illusion: four 700 g cubes, 125 cm3 and 1000 cm3; small rated
+0.81 z and large -0.81 z, partial eta squared .98, unchanged over 40 lifts and identical for metal
and polystyrene surfaces (Buckingham & Goodale 2013). It also occurs with objects resting on a
supported hand and when size is seen only after lifting begins. Material-weight: the object that
looks dense feels lighter at equal mass, and touch cues to material outweigh visual ones (Naylor,
Proulx & Buckingham 2021). Darker objects feel heavier.
*Accessibility:* a kitchen scale and a slicer infill setting. *Magic relevance:* prove ordinariness.
A gimmick's extra mass can be hidden numerically (under 5%) or perceptually (make the object look
denser, or a little larger).
Refs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weber%E2%80%93Fechner_law ;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3553013 ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Size%E2%80%93weight_illusion ;
https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13414-021-02414-x ;
https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/20169

**D2. Inertia tensor and moving masses.** Wielded objects are judged for heaviness, length and
asymmetry by their moments and products of inertia, not just mass. HCI devices move an internal
weight to change the felt object: Shifty (Zenner & Krüger 2017, IEEE TVCG), Transcalibur (CHI
2019), SWISH (UIST 2019, simulates sloshing liquid), and a 2023 visuo-haptic shape model.
*Accessibility:* hobbyist; a gear motor, leadscrew and tungsten slug for about $15 to $30.
*Magic relevance:* transformation and physical anomaly felt in the spectator's own hand, with a
scale as honest witness that mass is unchanged.
Refs: https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F3544548.3580724 ;
https://meteor.ame.asu.edu/publications/p751-sagheb.pdf (both seen in search only).

**D3. Illusory force and touch.** Asymmetric vibration of a small actuator produces a felt pull in
one direction with no external link (Traxion, Rekimoto, UIST 2013). The hanger reflex produces
involuntary rotation of the head, wrist or waist from skin shear (Kajimoto group; weaker on rounder
heads). The cutaneous rabbit puts felt taps on untouched skin between two tapped sites. Two-point
thresholds: fingertip 2 to 8 mm, palm 8 to 12 mm, back or shin 30 to 40 mm. Thermal grill: bars at
20 and 40 degrees C together feel burning hot "for some people". Spinning relaxed hands then
bringing palms together gives a felt repulsion.
*Magic relevance:* "PK touch" style effects. Wide thresholds on the back and shoulder mean a touch
there is poorly localised and poorly counted. Mid-air haptics and muscle stimulation belong to
agent 11.
Refs: https://lab.rekimoto.org/projects/traxion/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanger_reflex ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tactile_illusion ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-point_discrimination ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_grill_illusion

**D4. Body ownership.** Rubber hand: synchronous stroking of a hidden real hand and a visible fake;
correlated with hypnotic suggestibility and expectation (Lush 2019, 2020; disputed by Slater &
Ehrsson 2022). Marble hand (Senna et al. 2014, *PLOS ONE*): hammer taps every 1.2 s with a
marble-impact sound for 5 minutes made the hand feel stiffer, heavier and less sensitive (N = 46),
with a skin-conductance rise from 0.56 to 0.81 microsiemens. *Magic relevance:* strong for one
seated person given several minutes; an installation or séance piece, not a trick.
Refs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_transfer_illusion ;
https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0091688

**D5. Other touch illusions named in the task, by live usefulness.**
- *Pseudo-haptics* (Lécuyer and others): changing the ratio between hand movement and what is seen
  to move makes an object feel heavier, stiffer or stickier. It needs a display that shows a
  proxy of the hand, so it is a screen or VR technique. A physical analogue exists: a prop whose
  visible part moves less than the hand that drives it (a geared slider or winch) feels heavier
  than it is. (From memory, unverified.)
- *Velvet hand:* rubbing the palms together with a taut wire grid between them gives a smooth,
  velvety or oily film sensation. Works on most people within seconds, needs only a printed frame
  with wires or a tennis-racket-like grid. A self-working curiosity; useful as a "feel the
  invisible silk" moment, level 1. (From memory, unverified.)
- *Phantom touch and "PK touch":* Banachek's Psychokinetic Touches makes a spectator feel a touch
  that an onlooker saw delivered to someone else. The science that bears on it is poor tactile
  localisation away from the hands (D3), tactile suggestion, and reports of felt touch from seen
  touch in VR (unverified). A built aid is plausible: a silent, soft actuator in a chair back or
  collar gives the real contact so the performer's hands are provably elsewhere.
- *Cutaneous rabbit and funnelling:* two or three small vibration motors under a wristband or in a
  chair arm produce felt taps at skin sites where there is no actuator. Timing in the tens of
  milliseconds matters and I could not confirm the window. Hobbyist-buildable for about $10.
- *Thermal grill:* see concept 11. Reliability across people is the weak point; the Wikipedia
  summary says only "for some people".

### E. Vision

**E1. Acuity limits.** Resolution about 1 arcminute; vernier misalignment about 8 arcseconds; a
single high-contrast line is detectable at about 0.5 arcsecond because detection depends on
contrast, not width; acuity falls as 2/(2 + E) with eccentricity E in degrees. Converted: at 0.5 m,
0.15 mm resolution and 0.02 mm vernier; at 3 m, 0.9 mm and 0.12 mm. Ten degrees off fixation,
resolution is one sixth.
Ref: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_acuity

**E2. Colour and lighting.** A just noticeable difference is about Delta E*ab 2.3 side by side;
commercial print tolerance is 2 to 5. Illuminant metamerism: two surfaces match under one spectrum
and differ under another, worst under peaky spectra. The Dress: 57% blue/black, 30% white/gold, 11%
blue/brown (N = 1,400), individually stable; adding a yellow or blue illumination cue moved almost
everyone to the same reading. *Magic relevance:* duplicates and marked surfaces. Optics hardware is
agent 5's.
Refs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_difference ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamerism_(color) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_dress

**E3. Temporal limits.** Flicker fuses at 48 to 80 Hz depending on brightness (about 15 Hz in
rod vision). Stroboscopic effects are visible from 80 to 2,000 Hz, the phantom array during eye
movements up to 2,000 to 6,500 Hz. A cyclic motion lit at its own frequency looks frozen; one hertz
off, it creeps. Jeff Lieberman's "Slow Dance" frame is the known consumer product (Kickstarter page
returned 403; unverified).
Refs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flicker_fusion_threshold ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stroboscopic_effect

**E4. Depth-reversal and single-viewpoint objects.** Hollow face and the paper dragon survive
binocular viewing and appear to turn at about twice the viewer's own movement within a 90 degree
zone. Patrick Hughes's reverspectives use the same reversal on truncated pyramids. Sugihara's
ambiguous cylinders, "impossible motion" slopes, triply ambiguous object and 3D Schroeder staircase
(Best Illusion of the Year firsts in 2010, 2013, 2018, 2020) depend on a chosen viewpoint and are
usually shown on video or with a mirror. Ames window: a flat trapezoid seen as an oscillating
rectangle. *Accessibility:* all printable for a few dollars. *Magic relevance:* level 1 only, and
recognised as "an illusion". Their engineering lesson is the transferable part: viewpoint control
turns one shape into another.
Refs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollow-Face_illusion ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Hughes_(artist) ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kokichi_Sugihara ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ames_trapezoid

**E5. Static-image motion and fading.** Peripheral drift (Rotating Snakes) moves in the periphery
and with blinks, at a speed tied to microsaccade rate. Motion-induced blindness removes static
targets after about 10 s of fixation.
Refs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripheral_drift_illusion ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion-induced_blindness

**E6. Other visual effects named in the task, by live usefulness.**
- *Anamorphosis:* an image or printed sculpture that resolves from one viewpoint or in a
  cylindrical mirror. Robust for everyone, trivial to print, but it works for one seat. Good as a
  reveal for a single chosen spectator ("stand here"), or for a camera. A stage version needs the
  audience inside a narrow cone.
- *Lighting metamerism and colour constancy:* covered in E2. A physical object that changes colour
  under a different lamp spectrum is reliable for all colour-normal viewers and survives noise and
  distance. Neodymium glass, which shifts between lilac and blue-grey under different lamps, is
  the classic material (from memory, unverified). Build: a lamp with two "white" LED mixes that
  look the same on a white card. Hardware detail is agent 5's.
- *Strobe and temporal aliasing:* covered in E3. Works for a whole room in dim light when the
  strobe is above flicker fusion and the object's motion is periodic (vibrating string, dripping
  water, spinning disc). Fails if viewers wave a hand through the beam or film it.
- *Persistence of vision:* a single moving column of LEDs paints an image in the air; robust and
  cheap, but universally recognised as a gadget.
- *Saccade-only displays:* a stationary column of LEDs flashing image columns at kilohertz rates
  is seen as a picture only during an eye movement across it. Known from research and art
  installations (from memory, unverified); the physics is the phantom-array effect, visible to
  2 kHz and beyond. Different viewers see it at different moments, so it is unreliable as a shared
  effect and interesting as a private channel (concept 4).
- *Afterimages:* a bright flash in a dark room leaves a positive afterimage for seconds; an
  adapting colour patch leaves a complementary one. Reliable in a blackout, useless in ambient
  light. A camera-flash unit behind a stencil gives a séance audience an image that "stays in the
  air" and moves with their eyes.
- *Motion aftereffect:* 20 to 30 s staring at a rotating spiral makes the next thing looked at
  swell or shrink. Near-universal and already used by magicians as the "growing head" spiral
  (from memory, unverified). A motorised or hand-spun printed disc is enough.
- *Ames room:* robust but architectural; a tabletop version works only through a peephole.

### F. Hearing and cross-modal

**F1. Where a sound seems to come from.** Minimum audible angle about 1 degree in front, 15 degrees
at the side; elevation is resolved only for complex sounds with energy above about 7 kHz; front and
back share the same interaural cues; below about 80 Hz localisation is "difficult or impossible".
Precedence effect: when the same sound arrives from two places within 1 to 5 ms (clicks) or up to
40 ms (speech, music), it is heard as one sound at the earlier source, even when the later one is
up to 10 dB louder (best at 10 to 30 ms).
Refs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_localization ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precedence_effect

**F2. Audio-visual binding.** Sync error becomes detectable at 45 ms audio lead or 125 ms audio
lag (ITU-R BT.1359, expert viewers). McGurk fusion tolerates audio lagging by about 180 to 240 ms
but only 60 ms of lead, survives knowledge, and is weaker in Japanese and Chinese listeners and for
familiar faces.
Refs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio-to-video_synchronization ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McGurk_effect

**F3. Sound changing the felt body.** Marble hand (D4). Parchment-skin illusion (Jousmäki & Hari
1998) and footstep-sound body-weight work (Tajadura-Jiménez) are from memory (unverified).

**F4. Other auditory and cross-modal effects named in the task.**
- *Ventriloquism effect:* vision captures the location of a synchronous sound across a
  substantial angle (I recall roughly 20 to 30 degrees, unverified). It is the working principle
  of concept 3, and with F1 and F2 it sets the rules: same azimuth if possible, sound within
  about 100 ms of the visible event, something visibly moving at the claimed source.
- *Sound-induced flash:* one flash with two beeps is seen as two flashes (Shams and colleagues,
  2000; from memory, unverified). The time window is around 100 ms and the effect is strongest in
  the periphery. A prop could make one LED blink look like two, which has little conjuring value.
- *Shepard and Risset tones:* an endlessly rising or falling pitch. Completely reliable over any
  loudspeaker, survives noise, costs nothing. Useful as a tension bed under a slow effect, and as
  cover: a continuous glide masks small motor sounds better than silence does.
- *Binaural and ASMR localisation:* convincing close whispers and movement around the head, but
  only on headphones and best with individual head-related filtering. A one-person headphone
  séance works; a room does not. Loudspeaker delivery is agent 11's slice.
- *Parchment skin and marble hand:* F3 and D4. They need the participant's own action or the
  performer's tapping to be replaced by altered sound for several minutes.

### G. Suggestion, ideomotor action, placebo devices

**G1. Ideomotor.** Carpenter 1852; Faraday's 1853 table-turning apparatus; Chevreul's pendulum. A
motion-capture study (Cantergi et al. 2021) found the pendulum swings when the fingers oscillate
near its resonant frequency, so very small arm movement gives large swing. Ouija: answers beat
guessing on questions people believed they were guessing (Gauchou, Rensink & Fels 2012) and gaze
predicts planchette direction (Andersen et al. 2018); it fails when all sitters are blindfolded.
Harvard Group Scale mean is 5 of 12 with motor items easiest. I could not verify a responder
percentage for the pendulum.
Refs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideomotor_phenomenon ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouija ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypnotic_susceptibility

**G2. Placebo machines and atmospheres.** Olson's sham scanner (C5). Infrasound: a 17 Hz tone in a
concert of about 700 people gave 22% reporting unease or chills; Tandy's 19 Hz "ghost" was traced
to a fan.
Ref: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrasound

**G3. Table tilting and the dark-room séance.** Faraday showed in 1853 that sitters push the table
without knowing it. From memory, Wiseman, Greening & Smith (2003) ran fake séances in which about
a third of sitters reported that a stationary table had moved after the medium said it was moving
(unverified). The pattern matches the key-bending data in C1. For a builder the lesson is the same
as for the pendulum (G1): the mechanism only has to start a movement or supply a small real cue,
and a group's ideomotor response and reports do the rest. A table on low-friction feet with a
slight preferred direction is the classic aid.

## 3. Effect concepts

Each concept uses a perceptual or cognitive effect as the main method, with fabrication making it
reliable. The level tag says how the effect is used: 1, the illusion itself is the effect; 2, the
illusion is a hidden input that removes a weakness from a classical method; 3, a method structure
that depends on the illusion and that the audience never suspects. Eight of eleven are level 2
or 3.

**1. Inertia-matched gimmicks (level 2).**
*Effect:* none of its own; any electronic or loaded prop survives handling next to its honest twin.
*Method:* a script takes the CAD of the honest object and the gimmicked one and solves infill
density and a few grams of tungsten putty so mass is within 5%, centre of mass within about a
millimetre, and principal moments within a tolerance to be found by test. Finish as a dense-looking
material so residual extra mass is expected. *BOM:* printer, 0.01 g scale, tungsten putty ($10),
bifilar pendulum rig from string to measure moments. *Difficulty:* low to medium, mostly software.
*Risks:* the tolerance on moments is not known from the literature; needs a small blind test.
*Novelty:* I found no magic source treating the inertia tensor as a design target. Likely new as a
stated practice.

**2. The weight that a scale denies (level 1 to 2).**
*Effect:* a spectator hefts a sealed box that becomes distinctly heavier while it sits on their
palm; a kitchen scale on the table reads the same number before and after.
*Method:* internal slug driven along a leadscrew moves the centre of mass away from the palm and
raises the moment of inertia. The scale cancels "something was added". *BOM:* N20 gear motor,
leadscrew, 60 to 100 g tungsten or steel slug, small microcontroller, LiPo; about $30.
*Difficulty:* medium. *Risks:* motor noise and reaction torque during the move (move slowly, mask
with talk); the effect needs the box to be wielded, not just rested. *Novelty:* descends from the
Light and Heavy Chest; the scale-as-convincer and inertia method are new as far as I can tell.

**3. Honest bell under a borrowed glass (level 2).**
*Effect:* an ordinary bell under an upturned glass rings in answer to questions. Bell, glass and
table top are examined.
*Method:* the sound comes from a second bell and striker inside the table pad directly below. Same
azimuth, so only elevation separates the two, and elevation hearing is poor. The clapper of the
visible bell is nudged by a small coil so vision captures the sound. If needed a tiny exciter in
the pad fires 10 to 30 ms before a louder hidden sounder (precedence). *BOM:* solenoid, bell, coil,
microcontroller, pad; about $40. *Difficulty:* medium. *Risks:* a listener with an ear at table
level; the glass should visibly damp a real bell, which is actually a convincer. *Novelty:* spirit
bells are old; using measured localisation limits to make every visible part honest is the
improvement.

**4. Saccade-coded cue light (level 3, speculative).**
*Effect:* none visible. The performer learns a 4 or 5 bit code (a suit and value over two flicks,
or one of 16 objects) from what looks like a steady power LED on any object in the room.
*Method:* the LED is pulsed with a bit pattern at 1 to 2 kHz. With steady eyes it is a constant
light. When the performer flicks gaze across it at about 300 degrees per second the pattern is
smeared into a dashed line about 1.5 degrees long that can be read. *BOM:* any microcontroller and
an LED; under $5 on top of whatever senses the information. *Difficulty:* electronics trivial;
reading takes practice. *Risks:* spectators also make saccades and may notice a dashed streak (keep
it dim and near other lights); phone cameras with rolling shutter show banding. *Novelty:*
saccade-based displays exist as art and research; I found no use as a magician's cue channel.

**5. Slow creep plus suggestion (level 2).**
*Effect:* a borrowed-looking key on the spectator's palm keeps bending after the performer lets go.
*Method:* a shape-memory or bimetal insert moves the tip about a millimetre over 20 to 30 s, slow
enough that no movement is seen, only a difference from memory. The line "it's still bending" does
the rest: 40% report it with no real change at all, and one spectator asked to confirm aloud lifts
the others. *BOM:* nitinol wire or strip, body-heat or warm-hand trigger; under $20 (actuator
choice is another agent's slice). *Difficulty:* medium. *Risks:* overshoot makes it a visible
mechanical event. *Novelty:* the combination of measured suggestion rates with a deliberately
sub-noticeable actuator appears new.

**6. Prediction by choice blindness (level 3).**
*Effect:* the spectator freely chooses five photographs from pairs and explains each choice. A
prediction written and sealed before the show matches all five.
*Method:* for each pair where the choice misses the prediction, the hand-over delivers the other
photo. Johansson's lab did this with a double card on black felt; a printed card with a thin
magnetically held face that stays on a black tray does it mechanically. Asking "why this one?"
makes the spectator author the swapped choice. Three quarters will not notice a given swap; use
similar pairs and limit swaps to two or three. *BOM:* printed cards, 0.5 mm magnets or
repositionable adhesive, felt tray; under $20. *Difficulty:* low. *Risks:* a 26% detection rate per
swap is high for a stage; needs an out ("you changed your mind") and a room that did not see the
original pointing. *Novelty:* the principle is published science; a worked prediction routine with
fabricated double cards is not something I found.

**7. The decoy machine (level 3).**
*Effect:* a "neural reader" headband appears to pick up a thought. At the end the performer opens
it: empty shell, no battery.
*Method:* the real method is classical (peek, force, nail writer). The device is the false
solution; disproving it leaves the audience with nothing, and the disproved theory still blocks
the search. Run the other way for real electronics: plant an obvious sleight theory, then disprove
it with empty hands. *BOM:* printed shell, LEDs on a coin cell that is removed in view; $15.
*Difficulty:* low. *Risks:* some will assume a second device. *Novelty:* follows directly from
Olson's sham-scanner work; I know of no marketed routine built on opening the gadget.

**8. Seeded pendulum (level 2).**
*Effect:* a pendulum held by the spectator answers questions correctly, including ones only they
know.
*Method:* tune length to about 25 cm (1 s period) and low damping for maximum ideomotor gain. A
hidden reaction mass in the bob starts a sub-millimetre swing along the chosen axis; the holder's
own unconscious movement at resonance amplifies it. This rescues non-responders. For "things only
they know", use the unassisted mode, which reflects the holder's own expectation. *BOM:* brass bob
with coin vibration motor or tiny voice coil, button cell, receiver; about $25. *Difficulty:*
medium (miniaturisation). *Risks:* audible buzz; a seed large enough to feel. *Novelty:* unknown;
plausible that something similar exists commercially.

**9. View-cone occluder stand (level 2).**
*Effect:* an object sits on a thin stem in open space and is clearly isolated; a load appears from
or vanishes into "nowhere".
*Method:* the load rides directly behind the narrowest element, where space is perceived as empty.
A script computes, for a given stem and load, the cone of audience positions that are safe, and
shapes the load to fill exactly the occluded wedge. *BOM:* printed parts; under $10.
*Difficulty:* low. *Risks:* angles; two eyes see around a narrow stem at close range, so this is a
parlor distance tool. *Novelty:* the principle is old (body loads, pedestals); computing the wedge
per venue is new.

**10. The token that pulls (level 1 to 2).**
*Effect:* a spectator holding a small wooden token feels it tug toward the cup hiding a coin and
finds it themselves.
*Method:* asymmetric drive of a small voice-coil actuator gives a felt pull; an orientation sensor
keeps the pull pointed at the target the performer has set. *BOM:* voice-coil haptic actuator,
class-D driver, IMU, microcontroller, LiPo; about $40. *Difficulty:* medium to high. *Risks:* the
force is small and needs a light pinch grip; some feel only buzzing. *Novelty:* research devices
exist; no magic prop that I found.

**11. The bar that burns at 40 degrees (level 1).**
*Effect:* a metal bar becomes painfully hot in the hand, yet a thermometer never reads above 40.
*Method:* interleaved strips held at about 20 and 40 degrees C by Peltier modules. *BOM:* two
Peltiers, drivers, thermistors; about $40. *Risks:* only some people feel it; pair with suggestion.
*Safety:* hard-limit the warm side below 42 degrees C in hardware.

## 4. Dead ends and overhyped items

- **Motion-induced blindness, Troxler fading:** need about 10 s of steady fixation. No.
- **Rubber hand, marble hand, body swap:** minutes of induction, one person, tied to
  suggestibility. Installation only.
- **McGurk:** varies by person, language and face familiarity, and is recognised as a "video
  illusion".
- **Infrasound "fear frequency":** 22% reported anything in the best-known test; needs a large
  subwoofer. Atmosphere at most.
- **Mental priming force as a method:** 15 to 18%.
- **Sugihara objects, Ames window, hollow face, reverspective:** level 1, viewpoint-bound, and
  filed by audiences under "optical illusion". Use the viewpoint principle, not the objects.
- **Strobe-frozen motion:** betrayed by any eye movement below about 2 kHz and by phone cameras;
  flicker near 3 to 30 Hz is a photosensitive-seizure hazard.
- **Automating the riffle force:** the screen version dropped from 98% to 30%.
- **Peripheral drift prints:** nice on a card, but the motion is in the periphery and stops when
  looked at.
- **Putting the climax in the spectator's hand at high engineering cost:** not rated more
  impossible.

## Appendix. Perceptual tolerances for builders

The catalog findings restated as tolerances: what audiences cannot detect, will assume, or will
misremember, with numbers; source; method use. "Primitives" names the secret operation each one
helps (hold, move, swap, duplicate, conceal, transform, know, tell, delay, control choice, erase
evidence, prove ordinariness). This appendix is additional to the briefed format.

**Weight and touch**

1. *Cannot detect* a mass difference under about 5% between two objects lifted in succession (5 g
   in 100 g). Source: Weber fraction. *Method use:* mass budget for electronics inside a duplicate;
   about 5 g in a card box with deck, about 0.5 g in a large coin. *Primitives:* prove ordinariness.
2. *Will assume* the larger of two equal-mass objects is lighter, at full strength after 40 lifts
   and with knowledge. Source: Buckingham & Goodale 2013. *Method use:* a gimmicked object that
   must be heavier can be made a few percent larger and read as normal; an object can be made to
   feel changed by changing only its apparent size. *Primitives:* prove ordinariness, transform.
3. *Will assume* an object that looks dense (metal, stone, dark) is heavy, and will feel it as
   lighter than an equal-mass object that looks light. Source: material-weight literature. *Method
   use:* finish battery-carrying props as metal or stone, never as foam or card. *Primitives:*
   prove ordinariness.
4. *Will feel* an object as heavier, longer or lopsided when an internal mass moves, with total
   mass unchanged. Source: Shifty, Transcalibur, inertia-tensor model. *Method use:* a weight
   change a scale denies. *Primitives:* transform, erase evidence.
5. *Cannot localise* a touch on the back, shoulder or shin to better than 30 to 40 mm, against 2
   to 8 mm on a fingertip. Source: two-point thresholds. *Method use:* touches delivered by a
   mechanism can be off by centimetres and miscounted; never deliver secret contact to fingertips.
   *Primitives:* tell, conceal.
6. *Will feel* a pull from a sealed hand-held object driven by asymmetric vibration. Source:
   Traxion. *Method use:* a private signal channel that feels like an external force. *Primitives:*
   tell, control choice.

**Vision**

7. *Cannot resolve* detail under 1 arcminute: 0.15 mm at 0.5 m, 0.3 mm at 1 m, 0.9 mm at 3 m.
   Source: visual acuity. *Method use:* parlor props can carry 0.5 mm features freely. *Primitives:*
   conceal.
8. *Can detect* a high-contrast line far thinner than that (0.5 arcsecond, about 1 micron at 0.5 m
   in ideal conditions) and an edge misalignment of 8 arcseconds (0.02 mm at 0.5 m). Source: same.
   *Method use:* seam width is the wrong target. Kill seam contrast (matt black gap, no light
   leak), put seams on existing edges, and never run a straight printed line across a joint unless
   registration is under 20 microns. *Primitives:* prove ordinariness.
9. *Cannot resolve* more than half of foveal detail 2 degrees from fixation (17 mm at 0.5 m) or a
   sixth at 10 degrees (9 cm at 0.5 m). Source: acuity falloff. *Method use:* a tolerance map;
   quality matters within 2 cm of where they look. *Primitives:* conceal.
10. *Cannot detect* a colour mismatch under about Delta E 2.3 side by side, and are used to print
    variation of 2 to 5. Source: colour difference. *Method use:* target for reprinted duplicates;
    never show original and duplicate together if above 2.3. *Primitives:* duplicate.
11. *Will perceive* a visible front half as a complete object and the space behind a narrow
    foreground object as empty, against knowledge and across three viewings. Source: Ekroll group.
    *Method use:* only the visible hemisphere of a duplicate needs making; loads belong behind the
    narrowest thing on the table. *Primitives:* conceal, duplicate.
12. *Will miss* (54.5% on first viewing, 17.6% after previews) an unattended object moving in the
    open for 550 ms within 4 degrees of the attended spot. Source: Barnhart & Goldinger. *Method
    use:* an exposed mechanical action gets one performance per audience and half the room;
    actuators should replace it. *Primitives:* move.
13. *Will not notice* a change made across a blank of 67 ms or more, or made very slowly. Source:
    change blindness. *Method use:* a blink-length cover (flash, wipe, hand pass) is enough for a
    state change; slow actuators can work in the open. *Primitives:* swap, transform.
14. *Will see* an object leave the hand when it did not (63 to 68% with gaze cue, about a third
    with none), or an object never shown (32%). Source: Kuhn; Tompkins. *Method use:* mechanisms
    need only start an event. *Primitives:* move, conceal.
15. *Cannot see* flicker above about 80 Hz with steady eyes but *will see* it up to 2 kHz or more
    when the eyes or an object move. Source: flicker, stroboscopic effect. *Method use:* strobing
    tricks need either above 2 kHz or a still audience; conversely a kilohertz-coded light is
    readable only by someone who flicks their eyes on purpose. *Primitives:* tell, conceal.

**Hearing**

16. *Cannot tell* the height of a sound source lacking energy above 7 kHz, or front from back, or
    any direction below 80 Hz. Source: sound localisation. *Method use:* a hidden sounder directly
    below or behind the visible object is heard as the object. *Primitives:* conceal.
17. *Will assign* the whole sound to whichever source arrives first by 1 to 40 ms, even if the
    later source is 10 dB louder. Source: precedence effect. *Method use:* a tiny weak transducer
    at the apparent source plus a large hidden speaker delayed 10 to 30 ms. *Primitives:* conceal,
    move.
18. *Will not notice* a sound that lags its visible cause by under about 125 ms or leads by under
    45 ms. Source: ITU-R BT.1359. *Method use:* latency budget for any sensed-then-synthesised
    sound; wireless and microcontroller delays of 20 to 50 ms are free. *Primitives:* delay.

**Choice, reasoning, memory**

19. *Will take* the exposed card 98% of the time in a live riffle and feel free (about 90%); 52%
    for one of four positions; 45 to 54% for visual and classic forces. Source: Olson; Pailhès;
    Shalom. *Method use:* a known hit rate tells you how many outs to fabricate. *Primitives:*
    control choice.
20. *Will believe* their action determined an outcome it did not affect (93 to 96%), with or
    without misdirection or delay. Source: criss-cross studies. *Method use:* the mechanism can
    ignore the spectator's input entirely if the procedure has one step more than they track.
    *Primitives:* control choice.
21. *Will not notice* (about 74%) that the item handed to them is the one they rejected, and will
    give reasons for choosing it. Source: choice blindness. *Method use:* fix the outcome after the
    choice. *Primitives:* swap, erase evidence.
22. *Will feel* freer handling an object than naming one, and no more amazed by an in-hand effect.
    Source: Wincza & Kuhn. *Method use:* spend engineering effort on letting them touch, not on
    putting the climax in their fist. *Primitives:* prove ordinariness.
23. *Will report* a change that did not happen after one sentence of suggestion (about 40%
    against 5 to 16%), 60% if another witness agrees. Source: Wiseman & Greening; Wilson & French.
    *Method use:* supply a small real change and one agreeing voice. *Primitives:* transform.
24. *Will stay stuck* on a false method after it has been disproved. Source: Thomas, Didierjean &
    Kuhn. *Method use:* disproved sleight theory shields electronics; disproved electronics theory
    shields sleight. *Primitives:* erase evidence.
25. *Will accept* inert visible technology as the cause, and psychological patter as the method,
    whoever presents it. Source: Olson 2016; Lan 2018. *Method use:* technology as decoy.
    *Primitives:* conceal.
26. *Will misremember* procedure in the direction of the effect; about 30% build partial or full
    false memories under repeated suggestion, more with delay. Source: misinformation literature.
    *Method use:* recap the conditions in improved form before the reveal and again afterwards.
    *Primitives:* erase evidence.
27. *Will scrutinise* the start and end of a sequence and not the ordinary-looking middle. Source:
    Smith, Dignum & Sonenberg. *Method use:* schedule actuator events mid-procedure. *Primitives:*
    delay.
28. *Will disagree* with each other about an ambiguous colour (57/30/11) and then converge when an
    illumination cue is added. Source: The Dress. *Method use:* a prop whose colour is honestly
    disputed until a lamp settles it. *Primitives:* transform.
