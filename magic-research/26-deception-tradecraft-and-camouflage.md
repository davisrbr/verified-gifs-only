# 26: Deception tradecraft outside magic (espionage, military deception, camouflage, biology, cons, stagecraft)

Scope: fields that spent real engineering effort on magic's own problems (conceal, signal, swap,
misdirect, look ordinary), read as a source of capabilities and design patterns for entertainment
with the performer's own props and consenting audiences. This is a historical and design-pattern
survey. It contains no guidance for spying on, cheating or defrauding anyone, and the fraud and
gambling material is limited to structures and historic apparatus that are already public.

Research note: the shared web-search budget ran out after three searches, so almost everything
here comes from opening known primary pages directly (CIA Museum and CIA Studies in Intelligence,
Crypto Museum, publisher abstracts via Europe PMC, a Cambridge technical report, Wikipedia for
orientation). Anything recalled from memory and not confirmed on a page I opened is marked
"(unverified)". Prices are my estimates as of late 2026 and are marked as such; I could not check
supplier pages.

## 1. Summary

- **The most transferable idea in this slice is "design for what the examiner will do, not what
  they will think."** MI9's left-hand-threaded compass buttons and Fraser-Smith's left-hand screw
  caps survived searches because a searcher's hands only test one direction. A modern car "trap"
  opened only when four innocent conditions were true at once. Magic's examinable props mostly
  hide the seam; these fields hid the *action that opens it*.
- **Intelligence services solved "prove ordinariness" with mass, sound and backstory, not only
  looks.** The Hollow Nickel was caught because it "felt too light" and popped open when dropped.
  Later coins carried lead rings to match weight. Operation Mincemeat proved a fictional man with
  dated ticket stubs, a jeweller's receipt and an overdraft letter. Tungsten, thermal receipt
  printers and on-demand print make both cheap now.
- **The CIA's "Jack-in-the-Box" is the cleanest statement of a principle magic uses loosely:**
  create a short gap in observation by route, do the big move in the gap, and fill the vacancy
  with a decoy so the *count* the watcher is tracking stays correct. Magic's Asrah form (1902) is
  the same idea. Soft robotics lets the decoy be warm, moving and hand-held.
- **Whaley's taxonomy adds a planning grid magic's effect lists lack**: three ways to hide the
  real (masking, repackaging, dazzling), three ways to show the false (mimicking, inventing,
  decoying), plus maxims that are directly usable as design rules: reinforce a belief the target
  already holds, control the target's channels, never hand over a windfall, and deceive about an
  attribute (size, location, identity, *time*) the target is not yet watching.
- **Deception about time is under-used in magic and well developed in military practice.**
  Operation Bertram built a dummy pipeline at a visible five miles a day so the enemy could read
  the date of the attack off it. A visibly unfinished process is a lie about *when*.
- **Camouflage science quantifies things magic only asserts.** Outline-breaking contrast beats
  background matching (Cuthill 2005); a thing can be seen and still dismissed if the observer
  knows the innocent model (masquerade, Skelhorn 2010); high-contrast concealment is learned
  fastest on repeat viewing (Troscianko 2013); nothing hides a lone moving object, but similar
  moving distractors slow identification (Hall 2013); small eyespots pull strikes toward
  themselves (Kjernsmo 2013).
- **Inspection is a search task, and search for rare things on a fixed list fails in measurable
  ways.** Radiologists hunting nodules missed a gorilla 48 times nodule size 83% of the time
  (Drew 2013); rare targets are missed far more often (Wolfe 2005); TSA red teams got prohibited
  items through in 95% of 2015 trials. An examination with an assigned task is weaker than a free
  one, and this is engineerable.
- **Adaptive camouflage skins are mostly a dead end at prop scale**, with two exceptions: the
  silicone-and-mesh texture membranes of Pikul et al. (flat sheet to programmed 3D relief, hobby
  materials), and photocell-matched counter-illumination (the Yehudi lights cut detection range
  from about 12 miles to 3,000 yards for under 500 W on a bomber).
- **Con-game structure is a routine-construction manual.** The six-stage short con (foundation,
  approach, build-up, convincer, "hurrah", in-and-in) and Stajano and Wilson's seven principles
  describe how to stage independent confirmation and how to end so the audience leaves with a
  story that is not the method.
- **Variable-data tradecraft (canary traps, microdots, Stanhope lenses) points at an
  architecture magic has barely touched:** every "identical" printed thing in the room is unique,
  and a thousand outs fit inside a full stop.

## 2. Catalog

### A. Espionage tradecraft

**A1. Concealment devices and "wrong-way" openings.**
Hollow coins were made by milling two coins so one edge slides into the other; some were opened by
pushing a pin through a pinhole in one face, and lead rings were sometimes added to restore the
weight of a real coin (Wikipedia, "Concealment device"). The CIA Museum's Eisenhower-dollar
container is 4 cm across and 0.3 cm high. The 1953 Hollow Nickel was found by a newsboy because it
"felt too light"; dropped, it popped open on a microfilm of 207 five-digit groups. Its two faces
came from coins of different years, an anachronism nobody would have checked. The KGB used
hollowed industrial bolts; a VARTA 6 V battery case hiding an East German radio was found in 2018
roughly 60 years after it was placed (Crypto Museum). Fraser-Smith's document containers had a
disguised screw top with a left-hand thread; Hutton moved MI9's 6 mm button compasses to
left-hand threads after guards learned to unscrew them. A 2012 car compartment opened only when a
driver was seated, all doors were closed, the defroster was on and a magnetic card passed a hidden
sensor.
*Accessibility:* hobbyist. Left-hand threads print well on resin printers and are a tap-and-die
purchase in metal; a hollow euro-cent coin holding a microSD card is a catalogue item (Crypto
Museum shows one). Tungsten (19.3 g/cm3 against about 8.9 for coin alloys) lets a cavity be
weight-matched: fill 46% of the excavated volume with tungsten and the other 54% is free space at
exactly the original mass (my arithmetic). Battery adapter sleeves (AAA cell inside an AA shell,
AA inside a D shell) are a commodity part; a custom sleeve gives a cell that still powers the
device and is roughly half cavity (AA) or 80% cavity (D) by volume (my arithmetic from standard
cell dimensions).
*Magic relevance:* hold, conceal, prove ordinariness. Magic has shells, slot boxes and Okito
boxes; it has few containers that are *working objects, correct in mass and sound, locked by a
motor habit*.
*Prior use:* hollow "spy coins" are sold to magicians and collectors; I found no magic product
built on reverse threads or multi-condition release (not searched exhaustively).

**A2. Microdots and Stanhope lenses.**
A microdot is a page photographed down to a disc about 1 mm across; Goldberg's 1925 two-stage
process fitted a legible page into 0.01 mm2. They were hidden in letter punctuation, rings and
hollow coins and read with a small concealed viewer (CIA Museum; its microdot camera is 1.6 cm by
2.8 cm). Later dots used aniline dye rather than silver so they did not glint. The consumer
ancestor is Dagron's Stanhope (1857): a tiny rod lens with a 1 to 2 mm microphotograph cemented
to its flat end with Canada balsam, magnifying about 300 times, set into rings, pens and toys.
His factory made 12,000 a day by 1862, and one 1864 piece held 450 portraits.
*Accessibility:* hobbyist. Phone macro modes and clip-on microscopes read 1 mm images directly.
Making them is the harder half: 1200 dpi laser print gives legible text about 0.3 mm tall
(unverified), photographic reduction onto high-resolution film gives true dots, and Stanhope
lenses are still sold to restorers (unverified).
*Magic relevance:* conceal in plain sight, prove priority, brute-force outs at near-zero volume.
*Prior use:* Stanhope "peeps" are a Victorian novelty; I know of no indexed multi-out Stanhope.
Pointer: agent 23 for coding, agent 25 for thin concealment.

**A3. Dead drops, signal sites, short-range burst devices and cover traffic.**
The CIA's own account of the Tolkachev operation (Royden, Studies in Intelligence, 2003) is a
complete worked example. Materials were left in "a dirty construction worker's mitten". Readiness
was signalled by a chalk mark on a utility pole or by how a car was parked. A short-range device
exchanged several thousand pre-typed, enciphered characters in a burst "that lasted only seconds"
at "hundreds of meters", with the two parties at separate sites "far enough apart so that there
could be no apparent visual connection". One-way shortwave broadcasts ran ten minutes and
"included the transmission of any live message as well as dummy messages", so traffic volume
revealed nothing. The CIA dead-drop spike is 20.6 cm by 3.7 cm. Burst encoders go back to 1959
(CK-7) and East Germany's USG-1 ran at 500 baud (Crypto Museum).
*Accessibility:* trivial today (any 2.4 GHz or sub-GHz module; agent 10 covers hardware).
*Magic relevance:* the patterns matter more than the radios: (1) cause and effect separated in
time and person, (2) one-bit signals made from the state of ordinary objects, (3) *cover
traffic*: always emit, so the meaningful emission is indistinguishable, (4) the signal site is
somewhere the receiver passes anyway.
*Prior use:* stooge codes and impression devices are old; constant cover traffic as a designed
feature of a prop is not something I have seen described.

**A4. The Jack-in-the-Box and identity transfer.**
"A JIB (a popup device made to look like the upper half of a person) allowed a case officer to
make a meeting with an agent even while under vehicular surveillance." The car made "a series of
turns designed to provide a brief period when the trailing surveillance car would lose sight";
the officer stepped out and the driver triggered the dummy, which travelled in "a large package".
The same article describes leaving a compound disguised as someone else, then changing in the
vehicle into local working clothes, down to smell (garlic, vodka). Jonna Mendez's disguise lab,
building on Hollywood makeup artist John Chambers, produced masks good enough that she briefed
President Bush in one unnoticed.
*Pattern:* the watcher tracks an *invariant* (two heads in the car), not the people. Engineer a
natural occlusion, act inside it, and restore the invariant with a decoy.
*Accessibility:* a spring-steel or inflatable pop-up form is a weekend build.
*Magic relevance:* swap, conceal, delay. Asrah (Le Roy, 1902) uses a wire form to hold the shape
of a body that has left. The JIB adds: the decoy is deployed by a third party, the gap is created
by route rather than by a flourish, and the decoy is removed later as luggage.

**A5. MI9 and SOE escape aids.**
Hutton laminated mulberry-tissue maps inside playing cards that separated when soaked, inside
gramophone records and inside Monopoly sets; MI9 produced at least 243 map items and distributed
nearly two million maps (Bond, via Wikipedia). At least one map deck survives at the
International Spy Museum. Razor blades were magnetised so that, hung on a thread, they pointed
north, with the first letter of the maker's name marking the north end; a pencil clip balanced
on its dimple did the same. Gigli saws went into bootlaces (wire for 10,000). Flying boots cut
down to civilian shoes; a prisoner's uniform had a darker tailored lining that became a civilian
jacket. Fraser-Smith put cameras in lighters, film in shaving brushes and a map and saw in a
hairbrush.
*Patterns:* (1) the secret lives in the *glue line* of a laminated everyday object and is reached
only by an unnatural, destructive act; (2) the function is a *material property* (magnetisation)
with no compartment to find; (3) one object, two identities, converted by removing material.
*Accessibility:* hobbyist; card splitting and re-laminating is established gaff-making craft.
*Prior use:* reproduction map decks are sold as collectibles; I found no routine that uses the
lamination as an out system.

**A6. Magicians working for agencies.**
John Mulholland left *The Sphinx* in 1953, officially for health reasons, and wrote a manual on
deception and misdirection for the CIA; copies believed destroyed in 1973 resurfaced and were
published by Melton and Wallace as *The Official CIA Manual of Trickery and Deception* (2009).
From memory, it covers covert delivery of small objects and liquids, working in pairs, and
recognition signals built from clothing details such as shoelace patterns (unverified; I could
not open the text). Hutton's interest in escapology began with Houdini's 1913 packing-case
challenge. Jasper Maskelyne's account of hiding harbours and canals is not supported: Stokes
argues his role was marginal and that there was no "Magic Gang"; his experimental section was
judged unsuccessful by February 1942 and he was moved to troop entertainment. What is documented
is modest: MI9 lectures and escape aids, the first Sunshield prototype, and a hand in faking
damage at the de Havilland factory.
*Relevance:* the flow of ideas ran mostly from magic to the agencies. The return flow (budgets,
materials, engineering discipline) is what this report mines.

**A7. Backstopping, pocket litter and canary traps.**
Operation Mincemeat made a dead man real with a fiancée's photograph and two love letters, a
receipt for a 53 pound 10s 6d engagement ring, an overdraft demand for 79 pounds 19s 2d, theatre
ticket stubs and a club bill that together implied a London itinerary for 18 to 24 April, and an
identity card rubbed on trousers to age it. An eyelash folded into the key letter told London
whether it had been opened. The CIA's "Studio Six Productions" was a functioning dummy film
company. A canary trap gives each recipient a subtly different copy (wording, spacing, invisible
characters) so that any copy that surfaces names its holder.
*Accessibility:* a 58 mm thermal receipt printer is about $30 (estimate), variable-data print
costs nothing extra, a phone number or domain is a few dollars a month (pointer: agent 24).
*Magic relevance:* prove ordinariness by *corroborating incidentals the spectator discovers*,
and know (identify) through per-copy variation.

### B. Military deception

**B1. Whaley's structure and the deception maxims.**
Bell and Whaley divide all deception into *hiding the real* (masking: blend into background;
repackaging: appear as something else; dazzling: confuse) and *showing the false* (mimicking:
imitate something real; inventing: create something new; decoying: draw attention elsewhere).
Secondary summaries report the grid as ordered by reliability, with masking and mimicking most
likely to succeed and dazzling and decoying least. The maxims compiled for US doctrine include
Magruder's principle (it is easier to keep a target believing what it already believes), Jones's
dilemma (the more channels the target has, the harder the deception, unless you control them),
"avoid windfalls" (information that arrives too easily is distrusted), and a list of attributes
one can deceive about: size, activity, location, unit, time, equipment, intent, style.
*What it adds to magic taxonomies:* Fitzkee classifies effects; Whaley classifies *what is done
to the truth*, and insists every deception pairs a hidden real with a shown false. The attribute
list is a prompt for method design: most magic deceives about location and identity, little
about time, size or style. "Avoid windfalls" is advice about convincers: a proof the spectator
has to work for is believed.
*Prior use:* Whaley drew on magicians; magic theory (Sharpe, Lamont and Wiseman) overlaps but
does not use the maxims. Pointer: agents 1 and 4.

**B2. The Ghost Army: agreement across channels.**
About 1,100 men ran more than 20 deceptions in 1944 to 1945 using inflatable tanks, guns and
aircraft; mixed recordings of armour and infantry played from halftracks and audible about 15
miles away; and spoof radio. They also hung fake laundry. The inflatables were deliberately
camouflaged *imperfectly* so that reconnaissance would find them. Ukraine used drone-borne
recordings of vehicles the same way in December 2024.
*Pattern:* a false object is believed when sight, sound and incidental traces agree, and when
the observer feels they discovered it. *Magic relevance:* sonic and haptic "proof of presence"
for an object that has gone (see concept 4.9); deliberate imperfection as convincer.

**B3. Operation Bertram and Operation Titanic: repackaging, time and scale.**
At Alamein 722 "Sunshields" (hinged canvas shells) made tanks look like trucks and 360
"Cannibals" did the same for guns, while food was stacked in the shape of trucks. A dummy
pipeline with pump houses advanced at an apparent five miles a day and was left visibly
unfinished at the attack. The result was complete tactical surprise. On D-Day, "Rupert"
paradummies were only about 85 cm tall, indistinguishable from men at twilight.
*Patterns:* a quick-release shell that makes the important thing look like the boring thing; a
*visible progress bar* that lies about when; under poor depth cues an observer cannot judge
size, so decoys can be small.

**B4. Dazzle.**
Dazzle was never concealment; it aimed to spoil estimates of heading, range and speed. Over
4,000 British merchant ships were painted. The evidence is weak: a 2011 study found about a 7%
speed misjudgement, and only at speeds far above a ship's; a 2025 study found the "twist" on
perceived heading small. Touch-screen tests found stripes hard to catch but not uniquely so
(Hughes 2014). See dead ends.

### C. Adaptive camouflage research

**C1. Cephalopod-inspired skins.**
Morin et al. (Science 2012) pumped dyes through microfluidic channels in silicone to change
colour, contrast, pattern and surface temperature. Yu et al. (PNAS 2014) laminated photodetector
and actuator arrays under pixelated colour-change elements so a sheet sensed and matched its
background autonomously. Pikul et al. (Science 2017) embedded inextensible mesh in elastomer so
a flat membrane inflates to within 10% of a programmed 3D shape, imitating stones and plants.
Xu et al. (Science 2018) made infrared-reflecting skins. Kim et al. (Nature Communications 2021)
stacked patterned silver-nanowire heaters under thermochromic liquid crystal for a
colour-matching "chameleon" robot.
*Accessibility:* Morin and Pikul use castable silicone, a fabric mesh and a syringe or small
pump (fabrication detail beyond the abstracts is from memory, unverified); the others are lab
work. *Magic relevance:* modest. Colour-matching skins are slow, powered and
low in resolution. The Pikul membrane is the useful one: a flat, examinable-looking surface that
becomes a relief shape (a face, a word, a key) under low air pressure and returns to flat.
Pointer: agent 7.

**C2. Counter-illumination, projection camouflage, thermal tiles, e-paper skins, lenticular
"cloaks".**
Yehudi lights: forward lamps servoed by a photocell to sky brightness cut the sighting range of
an Avenger from about 12 miles to 3,000 yards; lamps up to 1.2 m apart merged at 2 miles; the
aircraft had to stay within 3 degrees of the line of sight. HMS Largs with about 60 projectors
was seen at 2,250 yards lit against 5,250 unlit. Midshipman fish halve predation the same way.
Tachi's 2003 retroreflective projection cloak works from one viewpoint only. BAE's Adaptiv uses
about 1,000 Peltier tiles (infrared only). E Ink Prism is a bistable colour-changing film sold
for architecture. "Quantum Stealth" is a lenticular sheet (unverified; the company page did not
load).
*Magic relevance:* counter-illumination is a $5 control loop (LED, photodiode, op-amp) and is
the only one here that solves a real prop problem: a support or thread reel that is dark against
a bright backdrop, for a narrow range of viewing angles. Bistable e-paper film gives zero-power,
zero-glow colour state (pointer: agents 5 and 7).

### D. Biological camouflage and its perception science

**D1. Background matching, disruption, masquerade, motion.**
Cuthill et al. (Nature 2005): high-contrast patches that touch the outline protect better than
background matching alone. Skelhorn et al. (Science 2010): twig caterpillars are detected and
then *misidentified*, and only by predators that have experience of twigs. Troscianko et al.
(2013, human observers): disruptive high-contrast patterns conceal best but are learned fastest
over repeated trials; "distractive" marks placed away from the outline are neutral or costly.
Hall et al. (2013): no pattern hides an isolated moving object; with similar moving distractors
camouflage slows *identification*. Coincident disruptive coloration runs a stripe across an eye
or joint so the feature reads as part of the pattern.
*Magic relevance:* rules for seams and gimmicks. Put the parting line under a printed
high-contrast edge that crosses the outline. Prefer a gimmick that is seen and dismissed as a
known object, and show the audience the innocent model first. Expect pattern concealment to
degrade with repeat viewing. A moving gimmick needs moving company.

**D2. Deimatic and flash displays, eyespots, autotomy, aggressive mimicry.**
Deimatic animals are cryptic until disturbed, then expose a vivid pattern (underwing moths,
mantises, cuttlefish). In flash behaviour the vivid colour shows only in flight and vanishes on
landing, leaving the predator with the wrong search image (this account of the mechanism is
from memory of Loeffler-Henry et al. 2018, unverified). Small eyespots draw fish strikes to
themselves without making prey more conspicuous (Kjernsmo and Merilaita 2013). A shed lizard
tail breaks at a pre-formed fracture plane and keeps wriggling. Aggressive mimics borrow a
signal the dupe has reason to trust (a cleaner wrasse's look, a firefly's mating flash).
*Magic relevance:* give the audience a search image the object will not have a second later;
place a harmless "tell" where you want scrutiny spent; make the discard keep moving; borrow
trusted signals (shrink-wrap, a tax stamp, a till receipt, a developing instant photo).

**D3. Open-water concealment.**
Transparent plankton pass 50 to 90% of light; hatchetfish sides are stacks of 5 to 10 guanine
plates at quarter-wave spacing, vertical so they reflect the same light a viewer would see
through them; 16 deep-sea species reflect under 0.5% (one 0.044%), cutting sighting distance
about sixfold against a 2% fish (Davis 2020). Countershading only helps when its gradient
matches the lighting: sharp under direct sun, smooth under cloud (Cuthill 2016).
*Magic relevance:* confirms stage practice with numbers. A vertical mirror vanishes only in
symmetric surroundings (the mirror-box condition). Black art lives or dies on the last percent
of reflectance. Shading painted on a gimmick is wrong the moment the lighting changes.
Pointer: agent 5.

**D4. Camouflage found by computer.**
Fennell et al. (J. R. Soc. Interface 2019) combined human detection trials with a neural
network to predict the least and most visible colours in a given scene from few samples.
*Magic relevance:* a method for choosing a gimmick's colour and pattern against a specific table
mat, jacket lining or card back by search, using volunteers or a vision model as the observer.

### E. Security evaluation

**E1. Inspection as visual search; security theatre.**
Wolfe et al. (Nature 2005): when targets are rare, observers miss them far more often. Drew, Vo
and Wolfe (2013): 83% of radiologists doing a nodule search missed a gorilla 48 times nodule
size, most while looking straight at it. Schneier's "security theatre" names measures that
change the feeling of security without changing security: random bag searches on the Washington
Metro produced no arrests in 18 months, and TSA screeners failed 95% of red-team trials in
2015. Seal assessments report quick low-tech defeats by trained people (pointer: agent 17).
*Magic relevance:* an examiner given a specific job ("count them", "check the seal is intact",
"make sure they're all different") runs a list and misses off-list anomalies; the *ritual* of
checking is what later memory records. This quantifies a standard magician's intuition and
suggests scripting examinations as tasks.

### F. Cons, cheating and séance apparatus (structure and history only)

**F1. Con structure.**
Edward Smith's 1923 stages: foundation work, approach, build-up, pay-off or *convincer* (a small
real or faked win), the *hurrah* (a manufactured crisis forcing a decision), and the *in-and-in*
(a confederate takes the same risk); some add a *corroboration* by an apparent sceptic. Maurer's
*The Big Con* (1940) gives ten stages for long cons, run in a "big store" staffed by shills.
Stajano and Wilson (Cambridge TR-754, 2009; CACM 2011) distilled several hundred scams
recreated for BBC's *The Real Hustle* into seven principles: distraction, social compliance,
herd, dishonesty, deception, need and greed, time. Their analysis of three-card monte: "the
sleight-of-hand element is actually the least important"; the game is "street theatre" in which
shills win to prove fairness and the mark is invited to cheat (a bent corner, a lipstick smear)
with the operator's unseen consent. Goffman's 1952 essay "On Cooling the Mark Out" treats the
ending as a designed stage (not opened here).
*Magic relevance:* routine architecture. Staged independent confirmation; the spectator as
supposed co-conspirator; the *migrating identifying mark*; an ending that supplies a satisfying
wrong account. Use only in declared performance.

**F2. Historic gambling apparatus as engineering.**
J. N. Maskelyne's *Sharps and Flats* (1894) documents marked cards, reflectors and holdouts at
length (pp. 73 to 111 cover holdouts, per Wikipedia), including the Kepplinger machine of about
1888, operated from the knees through a cord so that no hand movement was visible (mechanism
from memory, unverified). The engineering lesson is the actuation path: power and control are
taken from a body part nobody watches, through a Bowden-style line.
*Today:* PTFE-lined cable, 3D-printed pulleys and micro-servos make body-routed actuation
quieter and smaller (pointer: agents 21 and 27). Magic already owns this lineage.

**F3. Pickpocket attention management.**
Otero-Millan, Macknik, Robbins and Martinez-Conde (2011) tested Apollo Robbins's claim that a
curved hand path holds attention better than a straight one: curved motion drives smooth
pursuit, straight motion triggers a saccade to the end point and back. Pointer: agent 4.

**F4. Séance apparatus.**
Documented methods: slate messages pre-written, switched, or written with chalk fragments held
in fingers and toes (Slade; exposed 1872, 1876, 1882, 1885); "spirit" photographs by double
exposure, by a camera gimmicked to slide a pre-exposed image into place (Hudson, 1872), and by
plate switching, caught in 1922 when Harry Price X-ray-marked the plates; ectoplasm from
cheesecloth, muslin stiffened with starch, gelatin, and magazine cut-outs; reaching rods (a
collapsible ruler found in Houdini's cabinet in 1924, origin disputed; wire-stiffened intestine
reported in 1926); a "spirit" thumbprint in dental wax traced to the medium's dentist. Houdini
and Comstock answered with physical controls: a cabinet exposing only head and hands, boxed
feet, held hands. David Abbott's *Behind the Scenes with the Mediums* (1907) is the open
technical source.
*Magic relevance:* directly reusable in honest theatrical séance. Every item is a "control
defeated" story, and each control suggests a modern counter (concepts 4.5 and 4.8).

### G. Stage and screen neighbours

**G1. Quick-change.** Fregoli founded the form; Brachetti plays about 80 characters an evening
with changes in seconds, including a full change behind a tossed sheet before it lands.
Construction detail belongs to agent 15. Pattern for props: pre-layered identities shed in
order, each shed hidden by a natural occlusion.

**G2. Bunraku, kuroko, black theatre.** Three operators work each puppet in full view; black
means "not there" by convention, white is worn in snow scenes and blue at sea. Kabuki exploited
the convention by having a "stagehand" turn out to be a ninja. Black-light theatre makes the
convention literal with velvet and ultraviolet lamps spaced about a metre apart.
*Pattern:* an audience will edit out whatever the frame declares is infrastructure, and that
category is where both method and surprise can live.

**G3. Ventriloquism, forced perspective, trompe-l'oeil.** Alais and Burr (2004): vision captures
sound location when vision is sharp, and *sound captures vision when vision is blurred*; the
combination is statistically near-optimal. Forced perspective follows apparent size = 2 arctan
(h/2D); the *Lord of the Rings* crew slid set pieces in step with a moving camera to keep it
working, which only serves a single viewpoint. *Relevance:* in dim rooms, sound decides where
things are.

**G4. Sports deception.** Novice observers are fooled by deceptive body movement and experts are
not, yet both are *more confident* on deceptive trials (Jackson 2006). The basketball head fake
works because head direction is processed involuntarily, and it works better the more the
context makes the faked direction likely (Güldenpenning 2023). *Relevance:* establish the
honest pattern first; exaggerated fakes buy confidence, not only error.

**G5. Art forgery and what experts check.** Van Meegeren used real 17th-century canvas, period
pigments and a phenol-formaldehyde medium baked at 100 to 120 C, rolled the surface to crack it
and inked the cracks; he was undone by the synthetic resin and later by lead-210 dating.
Beltracchi invented "lost" works, pasted old dealer labels on the backs and staged a period
photograph with his wife posing as her grandmother; one tube of paint containing titanium white
ended it. *Pattern:* experts check a list of anachronisms and a provenance story; forgers win by
fitting what the expert already expects (Magruder again) and lose on one off-list material.
Relevance is to prop ageing for theatrical framing only, never to sale.

**G6. Hidden-information game design.** Social deduction games (Mafia, 1986, onward) alternate
private knowledge with a public phase and removed the referee over time. *Relevance:* a
procedure feels fair when the audience runs it themselves; pointer to agent 23.

## 3. Capability primitives

1. *Field:* MI9 and SOE hid compartments behind left-hand threads that searchers never tried.
   *Today:* resin-printed or tapped left-hand threads, about $0 to $20. *Before:* magic hid the
   seam, not the opening action. *Primitives:* conceal, prove ordinariness.
2. *Field:* KGB and CIA hollow coins matched weight with lead rings. *Today:* tungsten restores
   exact mass with 54% of the excavated volume left free; about $15 of tungsten putty or sheet
   (estimate). *Before:* shells and hollow coins are light and ring wrong. *Primitives:* hold,
   prove ordinariness.
3. *Field:* a smuggler's compartment opened only on four simultaneous innocent conditions.
   *Today:* reed switches in series, or a $3 microcontroller with an accelerometer, give
   AND-locks and gesture-sequence locks with no keyhole. *Before:* puzzle boxes, visible locks.
   *Primitives:* hold/release, prove ordinariness.

## 4. Effect concepts

Level 1 = the phenomenon is the effect; level 2 = hidden input to a classical method; level 3 =
new method architecture. All eleven concepts are level 2 or 3.

**4.1 The honest torch (level 3: working, weight-true concealment locked by habit).**
*Effect:* a signed, folded banknote vanishes. The small torch that has been lighting the close-up
mat all evening is handed to the spectator, who unscrews it; it is full of ordinary batteries.
The performer cracks one battery open and the signed note is inside. Torch, cap and remaining
batteries can be handled freely before and after.
*Method sketch:* one D or C cell is a printed-and-wrapped sleeve around a real AA cell, so the
torch works throughout. The sleeve's end cap is a left-hand thread with a printed "crimp" line;
tungsten shot in the wall brings it to the real cell's mass and kills the rattle. The note is
loaded by a standard switch and a pocket load during the walk to the torch. The spectator's
hands will try to open things the usual way; the instruction "check they're real, they light
the bulb" is an assigned task (E1).
*BOM:* resin print, shrink-wrap label printed from a scan, AA cell, tungsten, about $25
(estimate). *Difficulty:* moderate (label realism, thread fit). *Risks:* a spectator who twists
both ways; label gloss under close light. *Novelty:* objects-in-impossible-places is old; a
*functioning* weight-matched container opened by reverse logic is, as far as I can tell, not a
marketed method.

**4.2 The escape deck (level 3: brute-force outs inside the lamination).**
*Effect:* the performer tells the true story of the MI9 map deck. A spectator names any card and
signs its face. It is dropped in a glass of water; a minute later it peels into two skins, and
on the inner tissue is a fragment of map with a cross on the town the spectator named before
the card was chosen (or simply the card's own name in period typewriter face).
*Method sketch:* all 52 cards are split, a printed tissue interleaf is laid in, and the card is
re-laminated with a water-release gum. Each card carries a different out; the town is forced or
limited to the outs available, or the interleaf carries a full gazetteer grid and the cross is
the only variable (52 crosses). Outside, the deck is an ordinary used deck that survives
handling and shuffling.
*BOM:* two decks, inkjet tissue, dextrin or PVA gum, press; about $15 and several evenings.
*Difficulty:* moderate, tedious. *Risks:* thickness and snap differ from factory cards; humidity
may lift edges; soak time needs testing. *Novelty:* high as an out system; the peel itself is
historical fact, which is its strength as presentation.

**4.3 A thousand outs in a full stop (level 3: microdot index).**
*Effect:* a prediction card has lain on the table from the start, face up, reading "Look
closer." After a free choice from a large space (any of 1,000 three-digit numbers, or any card
and any position), the spectator puts their own phone's macro lens on the full stop. It reads
their number.
*Method sketch:* the card carries a 32 by 32 array of 0.3 to 1 mm microimages disguised as a
halftone border or texture. Only the dot over which the spectator is steered matters: a
sliding window in the card's holder, a sticker "to mark the spot", or a tiny Stanhope bead that
the performer sets down on the right cell exposes one image. A variant mounts 100 images on a
disc under a single Stanhope lens in a ring or pen; a detent selects the image.
*BOM:* high-resolution film output or photographic reduction, optional Stanhope lens; $20 to
$60 (estimate). *Difficulty:* high for true 1 mm dots, low for 3 mm "micro-print" read by
macro lens. *Risks:* indexing error; spectators panning to neighbouring dots (mask them);
"it's printed small" is not a method they can complete without the index. *Novelty:* high.

**4.4 Every programme is a fingerprint (level 3: canary-trap audience).**
*Effect:* parlour or stage. Audience members write thoughts on slips torn from their own
programmes, drop them in a bag and mix them. The performer takes slips at random and walks each
one to its writer, or names row and seat.
*Method sketch:* each programme is printed with a unique, invisible-in-context variation in the
tear-off region: letter spacing in a rule line, a border ornament drawn from a 10-bit set, or a
dot pattern. Programmes are placed on seats in known order. The performer reads the variant
directly or through a camera aid (pointer: agent 10). No pre-show work, no stooges, and the
slips are the audience's own paper.
*BOM:* variable-data print run, 200 copies for about $40 (estimate). *Difficulty:* low in
print, moderate in reading speed. *Risks:* people swap seats or programmes (then the effect
degrades to "whose programme"); keep the code human-readable under stage light. *Novelty:* the
"which seat" problem is usually solved with marked envelopes; whole-room variable-data print is
a broader, cheaper architecture.

**4.5 The control hand (level 2/3: Jack-in-the-Box for theatrical séance).**
*Effect:* dark séance, declared as theatre. Sitters on both sides hold the medium's hands
throughout and report that they never let go, while a bell rings across the room and a touch
lands on a shoulder.
*Method sketch:* the classic method is the false hand or the one-hand-as-two hold. The upgrade
is a silicone hand on a soft actuator: skin-temperature heater (thermostat at 33 C), a slow
pneumatic or tendon "squeeze back", a faint 1 Hz pulse from a coin motor. It is delivered from
the sleeve during the scripted moment when the lights go down and sitters re-settle (the
engineered gap), and it leaves the same way. The freed arm works a reaching boom.
*BOM:* platinum silicone cast, heater film, micro pump or servo, cell; about $60 (estimate).
*Difficulty:* high (life-casting, sleeve management). *Risks:* a sitter who slides up the wrist;
heater safety (limit to 40 C with a hardware cut-out); never use in a setting where anyone
believes the claims. *Novelty:* false hands are old; a warm, responsive one is new.

**4.6 Still developing (level 2: a progress bar that lies about when).**
*Effect:* the performer photographs the spectator with an instant camera and lays the white,
undeveloped print on the spectator's palm. The spectator then makes a free choice. Over the
next minute the print develops in their own hand to show them, moments before they chose,
holding a sign with the choice on it (or with a pale figure behind them, for séance work).
*Method sketch:* the "camera" is a shell around an instant-film printer mechanism and a small
camera module. The eject is delayed or a blank is switched so that the composite is printed
after the choice is known. The visible chemistry of development is the progress bar: everyone
knows it was "taken" before the choice. The film is genuine and can be signed on its border
and kept.
*BOM:* instant-film printer, shell, camera module, controller; about $150 (estimate).
*Difficulty:* moderate electronics, hard timing. *Risks:* the too-perfect problem is mild
because an instant camera is expected to be electronic, but the performer must not touch a
phone; composite realism. *Novelty:* medium. Phone "spirit photo" apps exist; an honest
physical print whose *development time* is the alibi is a cleaner structure.

**4.7 The lightning-rod box (level 3: decoy seam plus searched camouflage).**
*Effect:* any small "examined" container effect (coin in nest of boxes, ring in block).
Spectators examine the box hard and find nothing.
*Method sketch:* three camouflage-science rules applied together. The real parting line runs
under a printed high-contrast band that crosses the box's outline. A harmless feature that
looks like a method (a hairline groove, a slightly proud panel, a "screw") sits on the opposite
face to absorb scrutiny, as an eyespot absorbs strikes. And the band's colours are chosen by
search: photograph candidate patterns on the real box under performance light, ask a vision
model or ten friends "where does this open?", keep the pattern that sends most guesses to the
decoy.
*BOM:* printed box, UV print or water-slide decals; about $20. *Difficulty:* low to moderate.
*Risks:* Troscianko's result: high-contrast concealment is learned on repeat, so do not show
the same people twice. *Novelty:* the individual ideas exist in gimmick-making folklore; the
searched, measured version does not seem to.

**4.8 The mark that moves (level 2: identity transfer of the cue).**
*Effect:* an honest lecture-demonstration of three-card monte. A spectator, while the performer
ostentatiously looks away, puts a pencil tick on the back of the winner. They follow their own
tick and lose: the tick is on a different card, and the winner is clean.
*Method sketch:* the cards are prepared with a latent tick in slow-developing ink on one loser,
and the "pencil" writes in an ink that fades in two to three minutes (chemistry: agent 8). The
performer's only job is timing the patter. An alternative is a corner of shape-memory polymer
that relaxes flat at hand temperature while another, pre-set, curls.
*BOM:* inks or shape-memory film; under $20. *Difficulty:* low once the chemistry is tuned.
*Risks:* temperature dependence; both ticks visible at once for a few seconds. *Novelty:*
medium; the plot is the oldest in the game, the sleight-free delayed swap is the new part.

**4.9 Virtual contents (level 3: Ghost Army in a matchbox).**
*Effect:* a borrowed watch is locked in a small wooden box that the owner holds. They can hear
it tick against their ear and feel and hear it slide and knock when they tilt and shake the
box. It is then produced from somewhere impossible; the box, opened by the owner, is empty.
*Method sketch:* the watch never stays in the box. The box's false floor holds a motion sensor,
a small exciter and a linear resonant actuator. Firmware renders the watch: a tick loop, plus
impacts and slides synthesised from the box's own acceleration so that sound and thump are
phase-locked to the owner's hand. A moving steel slug on a short track adds the shifting
centre of mass. All channels agree, as in the Ghost Army's displays.
*BOM:* microcontroller with accelerometer, exciter, actuator, cell; about $30 (estimate).
*Difficulty:* moderate; the rendering model needs tuning by ear. *Risks:* lag over about 20 ms
breaks the illusion (unverified threshold; agent 11 covers haptics); weight of the missing
watch must be replaced. *Novelty:* high as a method; "rattle box" is a one-channel ancestor.

**4.10 The wallet of a stranger (level 3: pocket-litter prediction, personalised overnight).**
*Effect:* a "lost" wallet has been sitting in the venue's lost-property box since before the
show (the venue confirms it). A spectator goes through it: a café receipt, a rail ticket, a
library slip, a pencilled phone number. Each is dated and mundane. Read together they spell
out tonight's freely made choices, and ringing the pencilled number reaches a voicemail that
names the spectator.
*Method sketch:* Mincemeat's rule, "avoid windfalls": nothing in the wallet says "prediction";
the spectator assembles it. Choices are forced or limited by classical means, and the litter is
printed that afternoon on a thermal printer and ticket stock for the right date and town. The
voicemail greeting is recorded from the wings after the spectator is known (backstopping).
*BOM:* thermal printer, card stock, a second-hand wallet, a phone number; about $50 (estimate).
*Difficulty:* low technically, high in writing. *Risks:* invented documents must be obviously
theatrical props, never copies of real tickets or receipts that could be used as such.
*Novelty:* medium-high; the structure (distributed, self-discovered evidence with a live
backstop) is the contribution.

**4.11 The coin with no mark (level 3, speculative: information as material state).**
*Effect:* five genuine, date-matched copper-plated steel coins from the spectator's own pocket
change (after a covert exchange) or from a sealed bank roll are mixed in the spectator's closed
hands. The performer names which coin is under which hand, or finds the one that was signed
on a hidden sticker, without touching.
*Method sketch:* Hutton's razor blade. Each coin has been stroked on a magnet to leave a
different remanent polarity or axis. A magnetometer in the table edge, a ring or a pen reads
the field at a few centimetres. Nothing is added to the coins and they pass any visual or
weight examination.
*BOM:* magnetometer breakout, microcontroller, vibration motor; about $15. *Difficulty:* low to
build, unknown to make reliable. *Risks:* mild steel holds little remanence and loses it with
knocks; Earth's field and phones swamp it; only some currencies are steel-cored. Needs a bench
test before anything else. *Novelty:* high if it works.

## 5. Dead ends and overhyped items

- **Lenticular "invisibility" sheets.** By the published descriptions they smear a background
  along one axis and need stand-off distance; in a room they read as a ribbed plastic sheet
  with something blurry behind it (unverified first-hand; the vendor page did not load).
- **Colour-matching electronic skins (Yu 2014, Kim 2021) and microfluidic skins (Morin 2012).**
  Slow, powered, low resolution, lab-built. A reversible cloth cover does the job.
- **Infrared and thermal camouflage (Xu 2018, Adaptiv).** Audiences do not see heat.
- **Dazzle patterns to hide hand speed or direction.** The measured effect is small and needs
  speeds far above anything on a table; stripes were not uniquely hard to catch in human tests.
- **Retroreflective projection cloaks.** One viewpoint only; video-only at best.
- **Motion camouflage (approach along the line of sight).** Needs a single observer at a known
  point; magicians already move along the sight line where they can.
- **Deimatic startle as cover.** A startle buys a blink, but it flags the moment as important.
- **Maskelyne's wartime "illusions".** Largely his own myth-making; do not cite as precedent.
- **Numbers stations and one-time pads as stage methods.** Secure and deniable, but slow and
  solving a threat (interception) that a magic audience does not pose.
- **True photographic microdots for casual use.** A microSD card in a hollow coin stores vastly
  more and needs no darkroom; dots only earn their place when the *reading by eye* is the
  effect (4.3).
- **Ectoplasm.** Historically cheesecloth; of period interest only.
- **Quick-change of the performer** as a hobbyist project: years of costume craft; the idea
  transfers better to objects.
