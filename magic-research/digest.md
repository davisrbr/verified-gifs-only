# Report 01-magic-taxonomy-and-classical-methods.md


## Summary (01)
- **No taxonomy of effects is canonical, and the best scholars say none can be.** Fitzkee lists 19
  [1], Sharpe six (Britannica, seen in search only), Wikipedia 10 [5], a retailer 13 [8]. Lamont
  argues effects do not slot into natural categories because one trick can be framed several ways
  [2]. Use Fitzkee's 19 as tags, not as truth.
- **The more useful split for engineers is "happening" versus "spectacle"** (Smith, Dignum and
  Sonenberg 2016 [31]): an impossible *transition* seen for an instant, versus an impossible *state*
  held up for inspection. Sleight of hand is already excellent at happenings. Classical methods are
  weakest at spectacles (floating, sustained animation, an object that stays impossible in the
  spectator's hands), and that is where technology has most to give.
- **Classical methods reduce to about twelve secret operations**: hold/release, move, swap,
  duplicate, conceal, transform, know, tell, delay, control chance or choice, erase the evidence,
  prove ordinariness. Section 2.3 writes each as a spec with numbers.
- **The most common single complaint across all physics families is non-examinability.** Shells,
  key rings, gaffed decks, flap props, magnets in coins and thread hook-ups all work, and nearly
  all must be switched out before anything is handed over. "Prove ordinariness" and "erase the
  evidence" are the two operations classical magic does worst.
- **The second most common is the environment**: light (thread, black art, Pepper's ghost), angle
  (mirrors, palms, Balducci [13]), wardrobe (pulls and topits need a jacket [48][49]), seating
  (lapping), noise ("talking" shells, snapping pulls).
- **Information methods are the cheapest place to add technology and the least visible.** "Know"
  and "tell" have been done with verbal codes that took years to learn [24], confederates, and
  peeks that require the performer to touch the billet [29]. Any sensing-plus-covert-channel system
  replaces years of skill, and the audience never sees an object that could be electronic.
- **Every historical technology transfer that worked hid the technology in the building or the
  procedure, not in the prop, and wrapped it in a non-technical story.** Robert-Houdin's magnet was
  under the floor [3]; Pepper's glass was the proscenium [9]; Psycho's "proof of isolation" (a
  clear glass cylinder) was probably the control channel itself [47].
- **Audiences suspecting the technology is not new.** Robert-Houdin's Paris audience guessed
  electromagnetism in the 1840s, and he responded by adding a rope-and-pulley phase that a magnet
  could not explain [3][4]. That is the first documented "cancel the electronic theory" move and
  the model for today.
- **Mathematical principles are a huge under-used reservoir.** Their classical weakness is
  procedure (dealing, counting, a set-up that an honest shuffle destroys). A hidden computation
  that knows the state of an honestly shuffled deck removes the procedure and makes dozens of stack
  and parity principles usable under fair conditions.
- **New technology can enable new architectures, not just better parts**: hundreds of outs instead
  of four, methods that act before the performer arrives or after they leave, gimmicks that remove
  themselves, and remote "secret assistants" (the Mechanical Turk architecture [25] with software
  in the box).

## 3. Effect concepts: ranked classic method weaknesses most worth attacking with new technology (01)
As the task for this slice specifies, this section is a ranked list of weaknesses and the classic
effects each would unlock, in place of built effect concepts.

Ranked by (number of classics unlocked) x (how badly classical methods handle it) x (how invisible
a fix can be). Level 2 = hidden input to a traditional method; level 3 = new architecture.

1. **Non-examinability of the working object (prove ordinariness; erase).** Level 2-3. Unlocks:
   Linking Rings with every ring handed out, Chop Cup with cup and ball left with the spectator,
   shell-coin routines, Svengali-type miracles with a deck that is then honest, Zombie, flap
   props. Directions: gimmicks that lock into an ordinary state, gimmicks that self-remove,
   fabrication good enough that the gimmicked object *is* within tolerance of ordinary.
2. **Knowing the state of an honestly mixed set (know).** Level 2. Unlocks: ACAAN under fair
   conditions, Out of This World from a shuffled deck, identification of any card, every
   memorised-deck and Gilbreath effect without false shuffles, poker deals. One sensing capability
   upgrades a whole shelf of the literature.
3. **Knowing a thought-of item without touching the paper (know).** Level 2. Unlocks: billet
   reading, Q&A, drawing duplications, book tests with borrowed books, PIN and name revelations.
4. **Silent, invisible, rich cueing (tell).** Level 2. Unlocks: one-person second sight, any
   "know" device, one-ahead without a plant, instant-stooge and dual-reality structures.
5. **Invisible support that survives light and handling (move).** Level 2. Unlocks: floating bill
   and ring in daylight, haunted deck on the spectator's palm, rising card from a borrowed deck,
   animated handkerchief, spirit bell and pendulum effects in isolation.
6. **Hold and release with no grip and an off switch (hold/release).** Level 2. Unlocks: Miser's
   Dream with open hands, coins across in a spectator's hand, cups-and-balls final loads without a
   pocket trip, chop cup without a permanent magnet, PK-style adhesion that vanishes on inspection.
7. **Coverage instead of forcing (control choice).** Level 3. Unlocks: headline and lottery
   predictions with a free choice, "the object you named is the one in the sealed box", chair
   tests, confabulation. Hundreds of cheap outs, or one out created late, replaces the force and
   so cannot fail and can be repeated.
8. **Methods displaced in time (delay).** Level 3. Unlocks: an object that changes after it has
   been in the spectator's pocket for ten minutes; sealed predictions that become correct after
   sealing; haunted objects with no performer in the room; spoon bending in the spectator's fist.
9. **Switching flat objects in the open (swap), or avoiding the switch by rewriting (transform).**
   Level 2. Unlocks: prediction envelopes never touched at the end, signed card to impossible
   location, bill serial-number effects, torn-and-restored with the signed original.
10. **True restoration (transform, erase).** Level 2-3. Unlocks: torn and restored card or note,
    cut and restored rope, broken and restored thread or rubber band, each with the marked piece.
11. **Vanish without wardrobe or a lap (move, conceal).** Level 2. Unlocks: coin or ring vanish in
    a T-shirt, ring flight, vanishing silk and cigarette-type effects for street conditions.
12. **Visible change of a surface under scrutiny (transform).** Level 2. Unlocks: colour-changing
    deck, card colour change in the spectator's hand, writing that appears on a signed card,
    Spellbound. High too-perfect risk if it looks like a display; must end examinable.
13. **Chance devices that are fair on inspection (control chance).** Level 2. Unlocks: dice
    predictions, coin-flip sequences, spinner and bottle forces, Russian-roulette-style choices
    made safe.
14. **Light- and camera-independence for optical concealment (conceal).** Level 2. Unlocks:
    black-art productions at a lit table, mirror-box and Sphinx-type miniatures on a desk,
    installation pieces viewed from all sides.
15. **Safe replacements for fire and reagents (transform, erase).** Level 2. Unlocks: flash
    vanishes in venues that ban nitrocellulose, drinkable water-to-wine, any-drink routines in a
    clear vessel.

# Report 02-magic-market-survey.md


## Summary (02)
- **Electronics are a small, expensive, mentalism-heavy corner of the market.** In the Murphy's
  feed, 216 of 5,834 physical non-deck items (3.7%) clearly contain electronics. The share is 0.8%
  under $50, 14% at $100-200, 38% at $200-500 and 58% above $500. Magnets, by contrast, are
  mentioned in 6.6% of items.
- **Almost all commercial electronics sense something and tell the performer.** RFID cards, IMU
  dice, magnetometers, weight scales, colour-pen detectors and electronic impression pads feed
  thumpers, pocket screens, watches, earpieces and, since 2026, smart glasses. Electronics that
  physically do something to a prop (hold, release, move, swap) are rare: motorised thread reels,
  a few motorised close-up pads, smoke and spark devices.
- **The fastest-moving area since 2023 is the "reveal device":** e-paper hidden in chalkboards,
  wallets, luggage tags and key fobs; pocket thermal, ZINK and Instax printers; and a $1,750 ink
  plotter in a wallet. One maker's own copy says "a magic era flooded with E-Ink boards".
- **AI arrived in 2023-2026 as plumbing, not as an effect:** handwriting recognition spoken into
  an earpiece (PITATA, 2023), a card-detection model on the phone camera (Interceptor, 2023),
  live conversation transcription that extracts "a name, a date, a destination" and whispers it
  (Illuminati Whisper Studio, 2026), and photo-to-script (VIRT, 2026). There is also a visible
  backlash in product copy and on forums.
- **Hardware innovation has moved to Chinese makers** (PITATA, Iarvel, T-Tech, TCC/Infinity, Bond
  Lee/MS Magic, Bacon Magic, N2G, Bluether), with Illuminati (Australia), Electricks (Hungary),
  ProMystic and ThoughtCast (US) and João Miranda (Portugal) as the western specialists.
- **Ecosystem lock-in is the business model at the top end:** Illuminati's Unifi app, Electricks'
  PeekSmith/Atom, and ThoughtCast's Actions connect each other's devices and sell subscriptions
  ($99-299 per year).
- **Thread and levitation is technologically static:** motorised reels at $75-280, thread at
  $15-25, the Losander table at $800-4,000 handmade. The 2026 novelty is packaging (a MagSafe
  wallet reel, a modular smartwatch). No drone product appears in any catalogue searched.
- **Clear absences** across about 42,000 listings: zero hits for electropermanent or programmable
  magnets, piezo, mmWave radar, UWB, ultrasonic or acoustic anything, computer vision as a named
  feature of hardware, capacitive sensing, parametric speakers, computational caustics. Shape-memory
  alloy appears only as "memory metal" for bending, never as an actuator. Lenticular appears only
  as wall art. Thermochromics appear in one 2026 trick and one deck box.
- **Markups are large and the parts are commodity.** A $195-300 magnet detector, a $279 pocket
  peek screen, a $299 electronic die or a $454 Bluetooth scale each rest on a few dollars of
  sensor and radio. What the buyer pays for is miniaturisation, disguise, an app that talks to
  everything else, and a routine. A hobbyist can undercut the electronics easily and the disguise
  with difficulty.
- **Build quality is visibly limited by small runs.** Makers advertise moving from 3D printing to
  injection moulding as a selling point, sealed dice have batteries that die in "a few years", and
  ProMystic sells a $549 die with a non-replaceable cell rated for about five years.

## 3. Effect concepts: obvious product gaps (02)
Each of these is something I expected to find for sale and did not, across the catalogues above.
Costs are my estimates. Novelty is judged only against the commercial market; the DIY and patent
slices may know more.

**1. A magnet that switches off (examinable chop cup and coin set).**
*Effect:* a cups-and-balls or coin routine with the usual impossible loads, after which the cup
and coins are handed out and are simply not magnetic. *Method sketch:* an electropermanent magnet
(a low-coercivity and a high-coercivity magnet with a coil) in the cup base holds with no power
and flips state on a millisecond pulse from a supercapacitor, triggered by a tilt gesture or a
reed input. *Parts:* AlNiCo and NdFeB slugs, coil, H-bridge, small MCU, 100 mAh cell; $25-40.
*Difficulty:* moderate; the coil driver and getting a clean "off" are the work. *Risks:* residual
field detectable with another magnet; pulse click; heat if cycled fast. *Novelty:* high. Zero
market hits for the part.

**2. Whole-deck reader for an ordinary borrowed deck.**
*Effect:* a spectator shuffles their own deck and cuts; the performer names the card, its position,
and the next five. *Method sketch:* a small camera module in a card box, pen pot or phone-shaped
prop captures the faces or indices during a ribbon spread or riffle, and a card-detection model
(as Interceptor already runs on a phone) rebuilds the order and sends it to a thumper or earpiece.
*Parts:* ESP32-S3 camera board or an old phone, BLE earpiece; $20-60. *Difficulty:* moderate
software, hard handling design. *Risks:* lighting, glare on plastic-coated cards, partial
occlusion; needs a natural reason for the spread. *Novelty:* high for hardware. The market's
answer today is a $120-550 RFID deck that cannot be borrowed.

**3. Silent shape-memory actuation in thin props.**
*Effect:* a card rises from a deck in a glass, a sealed envelope flap lifts, a deck cuts itself,
with no motor noise at 30 cm. *Method sketch:* 100-150 micron nitinol wire as a contracting
"muscle" (about 4% stroke) on a flexure, pulsed from a thin LiPo; reset by a return spring.
*Parts:* Flexinol wire, flex PCB, 40 mAh cell, MOSFET; $15-30. *Difficulty:* moderate; stroke
amplification and cooling time (seconds) need design. *Risks:* slow reset, heat near paper, current
spikes. *Novelty:* high as an actuator. The market uses nitinol only as bending cutlery.

**4. Thermal after-image peek for borrowed objects.**
*Effect:* five borrowed coins or keys on a table; a spectator holds one for ten seconds and
replaces it while the performer is turned away; the performer names it. Nothing is gimmicked.
*Method sketch:* a low-resolution thermal array (32 x 24) under a table edge or in a prop reads
which object is warmer and reports by vibration. *Parts:* MLX90640-class sensor, MCU, motor;
$50-80. *Difficulty:* low to moderate. *Risks:* signal fades in 20-60 s; metal reflects; warm
rooms shrink contrast. *Novelty:* high commercially. Only SenSys claims ungaffed-object sensing
and its method is unpublished.

**5. A voice only one spectator hears, from across the room.**
*Effect:* a volunteer on stage "hears" the chosen word spoken while neighbours hear nothing.
*Method sketch:* a parametric ultrasonic speaker array (40 kHz carrier) in a lectern or lamp gives
a beam a few degrees wide at 3-8 m. *Parts:* hobby parametric speaker kit or a 50-transducer array
and amplifier; $60-200. *Difficulty:* low with a kit. *Risks:* reflections off hard walls leak the
audio; thin, tinny sound; safe exposure limits for ultrasound must be respected, and it should not
be aimed at ears at close range. *Novelty:* high. The market offers only bone conduction, which
needs skull contact ($200-1,249).

**6. Lenticular gaff cards and bills.**
*Effect:* a card held by the spectator changes face as they tilt it, or shows the performer one
face and the spectator another; a face-up card on the table is a different card from each side.
*Method sketch:* 60-100 lpi lenticular sheet with interlaced prints from an on-demand lenticular
printer, laminated to card stock and cut to poker size. *Parts:* custom prints at a few dollars
each in small runs. *Difficulty:* low. *Risks:* thickness and the ribbed feel fail close
examination; works best under a card guard, in a frame or at parlor distance. *Novelty:* medium.
Lenticular novelty cards exist outside magic; no dealer lists one as a gaff.

**7. Caustic prediction: a clear block that projects the answer.**
*Effect:* an ordinary-looking acrylic coaster or paperweight sits in view all evening. At the end,
a phone torch shone through it throws the spectator's "freely" forced card or word onto the table
as a pattern of light. No electronics, fully examinable. *Method sketch:* computational caustic
design (open-source solvers exist) produces a height field that is CNC-milled into acrylic and
polished. *Parts:* acrylic and machining; $30-150 through a CNC service. *Difficulty:* moderate;
polishing decides the image quality. *Risks:* needs a force or multiple blocks; focal distance is
fixed. *Novelty:* high in magic. Agent 3 or the optics slice should check prior art.

**8. Battery-free e-paper under glass: the card that changes in a frame.**
*Effect:* a jumbo card sealed in a slim photo frame, or a "sealed" prediction in a clear badge
holder, is seen early and is the right card at the end. *Method sketch:* the Infinite Tag
architecture, an NFC-powered e-paper panel refreshed by a phone or reader passed behind it, but in
card format with a printed matte overlay to kill the e-paper look. *Parts:* 2.9-4.2 in NFC
e-paper module; $15-40. *Difficulty:* low. *Risks:* e-paper grey tone and bezel; red takes
10-15 s to refresh. *Novelty:* medium. The parts are exactly what the market already sells in
tags at $250-310; the playing-card presentation is missing.

**9. Radar pulse reading without contact.**
*Effect:* a metronome, lamp or the performer's own tapping falls into step with a seated
spectator's heartbeat; later it "stops" when they are asked to think of a loss. Nobody touches
them. *Method sketch:* a 60 GHz vital-signs radar module behind a chair back or table front
reports heart and breathing rate at 0.5-1.5 m; a hidden actuator or the performer's thumper
follows it. *Parts:* 60 GHz module, MCU, output; $30-60. *Difficulty:* low to moderate.
*Risks:* needs a still, seated subject; several seconds to lock; fails through metal. *Novelty:*
high. Zero market hits for radar. A consent point: reading someone's physiology covertly should be
framed and handled with care.

**10. True object finding by ranging.**
*Effect:* an object is hidden anywhere in a room or on any of twenty people; the performer walks
straight to it. *Method sketch:* a UWB tag in the object and one or two anchors on the performer
give range to about 10 cm and bearing with two antennas; guidance by vibration. *Parts:* two or
three UWB modules, cells; $40-80. *Difficulty:* moderate (antenna delay calibration). *Risks:*
body blocking, tag size (about 25 mm with cell). *Novelty:* medium. ProMystic's Fifty Fifty does
a coarse left-or-right version at $499; AirTag-branded magic products do no tracking at all.

**11. Programmable-magnet props that behave "wrongly".**
*Effect:* two examinable wooden blocks repel until rotated a quarter turn, then lock; a ring
hovers at a fixed gap above a pedestal and snaps down on command. *Method sketch:* printed
multi-pole magnet patterns (sold commercially as polymagnets) give spring, latch and alignment
behaviour that single magnets cannot. *Parts:* $10-40 per pair. *Difficulty:* low. *Risks:* any
magnet detector exposes it; short working distances. *Novelty:* medium-high; zero hits.

**12. Thermochromic print on ordinary-looking paper goods.**
*Effect:* a blank receipt, coaster or the back of the performer's business card develops a
prediction while the spectator holds it, from their own hand heat, and it fades back to blank
before anyone can study it. *Method sketch:* screen or pad printing with a 31 C leuco pigment over
a matched background, or a two-threshold ink so the image persists. *Parts:* pigment and base,
$20-40 for hundreds of prints. *Difficulty:* low. *Risks:* cold hands, hot venues, faint ghost
image, UV ageing; inks are not food-safe. *Novelty:* medium. Pass The Salt shows the demand; the
print applications are not on sale.

# Report 03-prior-art-makers-academia-patents.md


## Summary (03)
- **3D-printed magic is plentiful but shallow.** What gets downloaded is re-modelled 19th and 20th
  century mechanical apparatus: coin slides and drawer boxes, thumb tips, pencil-through-bill
  guides, money-printing frames. The most-downloaded item I could confirm is a 2012 coin-piercing
  box with "almost 14,000" downloads; most others sit in the hundreds to low thousands. None use
  electronics, and none address examinability (layer lines and seams announce "printed gimmick").
- **DIY electronic magic has converged on one architecture**: a sensor hidden in a prop, a radio
  (BLE advertisement or ESP-NOW), and a vibration or peek for the performer. Published builds (a
  gyro die, a capacitive coin box, a free-fall deck) are mostly proofs of concept that stop before
  the performer-side feedback is finished. It is the same architecture the dealers sell, and it now
  appears in patents (Penguin Magic's tuck-box scale; Nathan Buzza's multi-vendor "Notifier").
- **The most honest maker write-up reports a failure of effect, not of engineering.** James
  Stanley's CNC-milled Bluetooth coin box worked and was fully examinable, and friends still
  shrugged it off as "rigged". A flawless hidden sensor does not by itself beat the "it's
  electronic" theory.
- **Academia has one sustained "computers design magic" programme** (Williams and McOwan, Queen
  Mary University of London, 2014 to 2016): genetic-algorithm and simulated-annealing design of a
  vanishing-area jigsaw and a stacked-deck phone trick, both sold in a London magic shop. Since
  then the literature I could reach is mostly magic as a *test* for machines (a 2019
  DeepLabCut-as-spectator study; a NeurIPS 2026 video benchmark) and logic formalisations of
  tricks. I found no paper in which software designs a physical gimmick, and none in which an LLM
  designs a performable trick.
- **Magic-industry patents are few and low-tech.** Google Patents returns only 109
  English-language families in CPC A63J 21/00 with priority after 1 January 2014, and many of those
  are toys, wands and Pepper's-ghost foils. The filings that are really for conjurers are small:
  magnets in half-balls and shoelaces, a thread-spool wand, a vibrating ring, a watch set by phone.
- **The sophisticated illusion engineering is in theme-park filings**, mostly Universal (Akiva
  Krauthamer and colleagues) and Disney (Lanny Smoot, Quinn Smithwick and colleagues): a sliding
  mirror-to-glass gradient that makes the guest vanish from their own reflection; mirrors that
  change angle while a camera waits for the guest to blink; an addressable electromagnet array that
  puppeteers a battery-less plush toy; props powered by a tracked near-infrared beam; an invisible
  UV stamp on the guest's hand used as a tracking marker; an infrared camera boresighted with a
  projector so beams of light appear to leave an audience member's badge.
- **None of that has been miniaturised for close-up or parlor magic.** Sensing the spectator
  (position, gaze, blink) and adapting the method to them in real time is the largest unclaimed
  area I found.
- **Technology-forward performers mostly make technology the subject**: screens, drones, robots,
  holograms, and now "AI". The best-documented case, Marco Tempest's 24-drone piece, turns out to
  rest on a classical method: the drones flew pre-programmed routes and the performer acted the
  interaction.
- **Theatre is the quiet success story.** Jamie Harrison and Chris Fisher's illusions for *Stranger
  Things: The First Shadow* earned a Special Tony Award in 2025, and *Harry Potter and the Cursed
  Child* runs a "#KeepTheSecrets" campaign. Methods are not public; the lesson available from
  outside is that technology is kept invisible and subordinate to story.
- **Adjacent consumer hardware already ships magic methods openly**: maglev lamps, chessboards
  whose pieces move by hidden magnets, sand tables, 3D-printed Sugihara ambiguous objects. The
  escape-room community has a mature free stack for hidden sensors (Playful Technology's
  Node-REDscape and RFID libraries) that magic builders appear not to use.

# Report 04-perception-science-and-engineerable-illusions.md


## Summary (04)
- **The most useful output of the science-of-magic literature is a set of tolerances, not a set of
  tricks.** It tells a builder how much weight, lag, seam, colour error, motion and inconsistency an
  audience will absorb. The appendix lists 28 of these with numbers.
- **Forcing is quantified.** A salience-based riffle force reached 98% on 118 people with about 90%
  feeling free (Olson 2015); classic force 54% and visual force 45% (Shalom 2013, N = 31); position
  force 52% for one card of four (Pailhès & Kuhn 2020); the Derren Brown style mental priming force
  17.8% against 1.9% chance (Pailhès & Kuhn 2020). Felt freedom does not track actual freedom in any
  of these studies.
- **A chosen object can be swapped after the choice and about three quarters of people will not
  notice**, and will then explain why they chose the thing they did not choose (choice blindness:
  26% detection for faces, and no more than about a quarter of swaps for jam and tea). This is a "swap"
  primitive that needs no cover, only a moment of disengaged attention.
- **Occluded space is perceived, not merely assumed, to be empty, and visible half-objects are
  perceived as whole** (Ekroll's illusion of absence and amodal volume completion). Tricks built on
  these two effects were almost never solved across three viewings, while misdirection tricks were
  mostly solved. Fabricated shells and narrow occluders are therefore the most repeat-proof
  concealment available.
- **Attention-based concealment is weak on repeat.** A coin moving in plain view for 550 ms was
  missed by 54.5% on first viewing and 17.6% after three preview trials (Barnhart & Goldinger
  2014). Technology should take over exactly the actions that currently rely on misdirection.
- **A false solution keeps working after it has been disproved** (Thomas, Didierjean & Kuhn 2018).
  This is the best experimental support for pairing technology with classical method: give
  the audience a sleight theory and then destroy it, and they do not move on to "electronics"; give
  them a visible inert gadget and then open it, and they do not move on to sleight.
- **Inert technology is readily believed.** Olson's switched-off scanner was accepted as reading
  and inserting thoughts; a "psychologist" framing was believed even when the performer was
  introduced as a magician (Lan et al. 2018). Visible technology is better used as a decoy than as
  a method.
- **Verbal suggestion alone changes what about 40% of witnesses report** (a key "still bending":
  39 to 40% with suggestion against 5 to 16% without; 60% with an agreeing co-witness, 23% with a
  dissenting one). A prop only has to supply a small real change for suggestion to carry the rest.
- **Weight is the best haptic lever.** The Weber fraction for lifted weight is about 5%; the
  size-weight illusion is very large (partial eta squared .98) and survived 40 lifts; felt weight
  and length follow the inertia tensor, which a hidden moving mass can change while a scale reads
  the same.
- **Most textbook illusions fail live.** Motion-induced blindness needs 10 s of steady fixation,
  the rubber and marble hand need minutes and correlate with suggestibility, McGurk varies by
  person and language, infrasound gave a 22% report rate. They are listed under dead ends.
- **Magicians' intuitions are measurably wrong in places.** In Wincza & Kuhn (2025) audience
  participation lowered enjoyment, naming a card felt less free than touching one, and an effect in
  the spectator's hand was rated no more impossible than one elsewhere.

## Appendix. Perceptual tolerances for builders (04)
The catalog findings restated as tolerances: what audiences cannot detect, will assume, or will
misremember, with numbers; source; method use. "Primitives" names the secret operation each one
helps (hold, move, swap, duplicate, conceal, transform, know, tell, delay, control choice, erase
evidence, prove ordinariness). This appendix is additional to the briefed format.

**Weight and touch**

1. *Cannot detect* a mass difference under about 5% between two objects lifted in succession (5 g
   in 100 g). Source: Weber fraction. *Method use:* mass budget for electronics inside a duplicate;
   about 5 g in a card box with deck, about 0.5 g in a large coin. *Primitives:* prove ordinariness.
2. *Will assume* the larger of two equal-mass objects is lighter, at full strength after 40 lifts
   and with knowledge. Source: Buckingham & Goodale 2013. *Method use:* a gimmicked object that
   must be heavier can be made a few percent larger and read as normal; an object can be made to
   feel changed by changing only its apparent size. *Primitives:* prove ordinariness, transform.
3. *Will assume* an object that looks dense (metal, stone, dark) is heavy, and will feel it as
   lighter than an equal-mass object that looks light. Source: material-weight literature. *Method
   use:* finish battery-carrying props as metal or stone, never as foam or card. *Primitives:*
   prove ordinariness.
4. *Will feel* an object as heavier, longer or lopsided when an internal mass moves, with total
   mass unchanged. Source: Shifty, Transcalibur, inertia-tensor model. *Method use:* a weight
   change a scale denies. *Primitives:* transform, erase evidence.
5. *Cannot localise* a touch on the back, shoulder or shin to better than 30 to 40 mm, against 2
   to 8 mm on a fingertip. Source: two-point thresholds. *Method use:* touches delivered by a
   mechanism can be off by centimetres and miscounted; never deliver secret contact to fingertips.
   *Primitives:* tell, conceal.
6. *Will feel* a pull from a sealed hand-held object driven by asymmetric vibration. Source:
   Traxion. *Method use:* a private signal channel that feels like an external force. *Primitives:*
   tell, control choice.

**Vision**

7. *Cannot resolve* detail under 1 arcminute: 0.15 mm at 0.5 m, 0.3 mm at 1 m, 0.9 mm at 3 m.
   Source: visual acuity. *Method use:* parlor props can carry 0.5 mm features freely. *Primitives:*
   conceal.
8. *Can detect* a high-contrast line far thinner than that (0.5 arcsecond, about 1 micron at 0.5 m
   in ideal conditions) and an edge misalignment of 8 arcseconds (0.02 mm at 0.5 m). Source: same.
   *Method use:* seam width is the wrong target. Kill seam contrast (matt black gap, no light
   leak), put seams on existing edges, and never run a straight printed line across a joint unless
   registration is under 20 microns. *Primitives:* prove ordinariness.
9. *Cannot resolve* more than half of foveal detail 2 degrees from fixation (17 mm at 0.5 m) or a
   sixth at 10 degrees (9 cm at 0.5 m). Source: acuity falloff. *Method use:* a tolerance map;
   quality matters within 2 cm of where they look. *Primitives:* conceal.
10. *Cannot detect* a colour mismatch under about Delta E 2.3 side by side, and are used to print
    variation of 2 to 5. Source: colour difference. *Method use:* target for reprinted duplicates;
    never show original and duplicate together if above 2.3. *Primitives:* duplicate.
11. *Will perceive* a visible front half as a complete object and the space behind a narrow
    foreground object as empty, against knowledge and across three viewings. Source: Ekroll group.
    *Method use:* only the visible hemisphere of a duplicate needs making; loads belong behind the
    narrowest thing on the table. *Primitives:* conceal, duplicate.
12. *Will miss* (54.5% on first viewing, 17.6% after previews) an unattended object moving in the
    open for 550 ms within 4 degrees of the attended spot. Source: Barnhart & Goldinger. *Method
    use:* an exposed mechanical action gets one performance per audience and half the room;
    actuators should replace it. *Primitives:* move.
13. *Will not notice* a change made across a blank of 67 ms or more, or made very slowly. Source:
    change blindness. *Method use:* a blink-length cover (flash, wipe, hand pass) is enough for a
    state change; slow actuators can work in the open. *Primitives:* swap, transform.
14. *Will see* an object leave the hand when it did not (63 to 68% with gaze cue, about a third
    with none), or an object never shown (32%). Source: Kuhn; Tompkins. *Method use:* mechanisms
    need only start an event. *Primitives:* move, conceal.
15. *Cannot see* flicker above about 80 Hz with steady eyes but *will see* it up to 2 kHz or more
    when the eyes or an object move. Source: flicker, stroboscopic effect. *Method use:* strobing
    tricks need either above 2 kHz or a still audience; conversely a kilohertz-coded light is
    readable only by someone who flicks their eyes on purpose. *Primitives:* tell, conceal.

**Hearing**

16. *Cannot tell* the height of a sound source lacking energy above 7 kHz, or front from back, or
    any direction below 80 Hz. Source: sound localisation. *Method use:* a hidden sounder directly
    below or behind the visible object is heard as the object. *Primitives:* conceal.
17. *Will assign* the whole sound to whichever source arrives first by 1 to 40 ms, even if the
    later source is 10 dB louder. Source: precedence effect. *Method use:* a tiny weak transducer
    at the apparent source plus a large hidden speaker delayed 10 to 30 ms. *Primitives:* conceal,
    move.
18. *Will not notice* a sound that lags its visible cause by under about 125 ms or leads by under
    45 ms. Source: ITU-R BT.1359. *Method use:* latency budget for any sensed-then-synthesised
    sound; wireless and microcontroller delays of 20 to 50 ms are free. *Primitives:* delay.

**Choice, reasoning, memory**

19. *Will take* the exposed card 98% of the time in a live riffle and feel free (about 90%); 52%
    for one of four positions; 45 to 54% for visual and classic forces. Source: Olson; Pailhès;
    Shalom. *Method use:* a known hit rate tells you how many outs to fabricate. *Primitives:*
    control choice.
20. *Will believe* their action determined an outcome it did not affect (93 to 96%), with or
    without misdirection or delay. Source: criss-cross studies. *Method use:* the mechanism can
    ignore the spectator's input entirely if the procedure has one step more than they track.
    *Primitives:* control choice.
21. *Will not notice* (about 74%) that the item handed to them is the one they rejected, and will
    give reasons for choosing it. Source: choice blindness. *Method use:* fix the outcome after the
    choice. *Primitives:* swap, erase evidence.
22. *Will feel* freer handling an object than naming one, and no more amazed by an in-hand effect.
    Source: Wincza & Kuhn. *Method use:* spend engineering effort on letting them touch, not on
    putting the climax in their fist. *Primitives:* prove ordinariness.
23. *Will report* a change that did not happen after one sentence of suggestion (about 40%
    against 5 to 16%), 60% if another witness agrees. Source: Wiseman & Greening; Wilson & French.
    *Method use:* supply a small real change and one agreeing voice. *Primitives:* transform.
24. *Will stay stuck* on a false method after it has been disproved. Source: Thomas, Didierjean &
    Kuhn. *Method use:* disproved sleight theory shields electronics; disproved electronics theory
    shields sleight. *Primitives:* erase evidence.
25. *Will accept* inert visible technology as the cause, and psychological patter as the method,
    whoever presents it. Source: Olson 2016; Lan 2018. *Method use:* technology as decoy.
    *Primitives:* conceal.
26. *Will misremember* procedure in the direction of the effect; about 30% build partial or full
    false memories under repeated suggestion, more with delay. Source: misinformation literature.
    *Method use:* recap the conditions in improved form before the reveal and again afterwards.
    *Primitives:* erase evidence.
27. *Will scrutinise* the start and end of a sequence and not the ordinary-looking middle. Source:
    Smith, Dignum & Sonenberg. *Method use:* schedule actuator events mid-procedure. *Primitives:*
    delay.
28. *Will disagree* with each other about an ambiguous colour (57/30/11) and then converge when an
    illumination cue is added. Source: The Dress. *Method use:* a prop whose colour is honestly
    disputed until a lamp settles it. *Primitives:* transform.

# Report 05-optics-photonics-displays.md


## Summary (05)
- **The most useful optical capability for a magician is selective visibility, not spectacle.**
  Cheap parts now let a builder decide, per surface, which observer (performer, one spectator, a
  camera, nobody) can read it, by polarisation, wavelength, angle or position of the light source.
- **Polarisation gives a private, full-resolution display channel for about $20.** An LCD with its
  front polariser peeled off looks like a blank white light panel; anyone wearing ordinary
  polarised sunglasses sees the full image. Google used a stacked version at I/O 2015. I found no
  sign that magic uses this as a prompter.
- **"Looks opaque, passes infrared" is now a filament and a print setting.** IR-transmitting PLA
  costs about $6 per 100 g; a 1.1 mm wall hides a code, sensor, LED or camera completely (MIT
  InfraredTags, CHI 2022). Likewise, black printed with carbon (K) and black printed as a C+M+Y
  mix match by eye and separate under near-IR, which banknotes exploit.
- **Paper-looking displays with no battery exist off the shelf.** A 2.9 inch NFC-powered e-paper
  panel is $27.99, has no battery, is powered and rewritten by a phone held against it, and holds
  its image indefinitely. It reads as print, not as a screen.
- **Displays can hide behind real materials.** Google's "Hidden Interfaces" (CHI 2022) drives a
  PMOLED through 0.2 to 1.6 mm of wood veneer, textile, acrylic or mirror film; the surface looks
  untouched when off.
- **Ultra-black paint moved from lab to craft shop.** Musou Black (about 99.4% absorption, EUR 25
  per 100 ml) and Black 4.0 (maker claims 99%+, GBP 49.99 per 150 ml) bring black art down from
  stage distance toward arm's length; the limit is now durability, not darkness.
- **Total internal reflection is a mirror with no silvering and no edge.** Inside a clear acrylic
  block, an air gap perpendicular to the viewing face is a perfect mirror at every viewing angle
  (critical angle 41.8 degrees), and it switches off when the gap is flooded with water. That
  allows the mirror principle inside an object that looks like solid clear plastic.
- **Caustic "magic windows" are hobbyist-buildable** with open code (Ferraro, MIT licence) and a
  hobby CNC or resin printer. They are level 1 on their own; they become interesting when cheap
  enough to make one per outcome.
- **Cloaks, aerial images and volumetric displays are mostly weak for live magic.** Each has a
  narrow working geometry, visible apparatus, and an audience that immediately says "hologram" or
  "screen". AIRR floating images are the most buildable (about $150 to $300) and the most honest
  about being dim.
- **Hazards in this slice:** lasers (anything scanned, anything above 5 mW, all 405 nm), UV LEDs
  at 365 nm, the 48 to 65 V AC drivers for PDLC film, and fragile glass optics near spectators.

# Report 06-computational-fabrication-3d-printing.md


## Summary (06)
- **The field offers two kinds of transfer.** Direct showpieces (shadow art, lenticular objects,
  asymmetric spinning tops, caustic windows, ambiguous cylinders) are buildable and catalogued in
  section G. The same papers also contain a quieter tool: optimisers that set an object's mass,
  centre of mass and inertia tensor through hidden internal voids while the outside does not
  change (Make It Stand, Spin-It, buoyancy optimisation). Used backwards, they make a gimmicked
  object match a genuine one in the hand.
- **A consumer printer now spans a 20x density range in one object**: foamed LW-PLA at 0.40 to
  0.48 g/cm3, PLA at 1.24, iron PLA at 1.85, tungsten filament at 8.0 to 9.2, and a 48x range with
  an inserted tungsten slug (19.3). Weight, balance point and "heft" are free design variables.
- **Chance can be biased with nothing to find.** "Putting Rigid Bodies to Rest" (SIGGRAPH 2025)
  computes the probability of every resting face of any convex shape with no simulation, and its
  inverse mode designs solid, homogeneous dice with chosen non-uniform probabilities. Code, a
  Blender plugin and STL files are public. Hidden weights in a normal cube give only a modest bias
  (my estimate: about 22% against 13% for opposite faces), so geometry is the stronger lever.
- **One-piece mechanisms remove the classic tells.** Metamaterial mechanisms (a door latch printed
  as one block), bistable signal-propagating cells (a printed combination lock), compliant flexures
  and print-in-place joints have no screws, no assembly seam and no backlash rattle.
- **Layer lines are camouflage.** A parting plane or programmed break plane that follows a layer
  boundary is one line among hundreds. Printed objects are also now socially ordinary (fidget toys,
  phone stands), which gives a gimmick an innocent reason to look printed.
- **"Black to the eye, clear to a camera" is a cheap wall material.** Infrared-transmitting PLA
  (about $5.86 per 100 g) passes about 45% of near-infrared per mm and 0 to 15% of visible light.
  A $20 NoIR camera reads through 1.08 mm of it at up to 250 cm. This is a hidden *know* channel
  through an apparently opaque solid wall.
- **Fracture surfaces can be duplicated.** A torn or broken edge is magic's standard proof of
  identity. Resin printers reproduce a designed "random" fracture surface on any number of copies,
  so the proof can be faked. I found no prior use of this.
- **Brute force is nearly free.** A 6 x 6 x 1.5 mm resin token is 0.054 cm3; a thousand different
  ones use 54 cm3 of resin, about $2 of material (my estimate). Every outcome can be pre-made.
- **Pause-and-insert makes sealed objects with contents that could not have been put there**:
  magnets, coins, NFC tags, steel balls, liquids. PrusaSlicer documents the workflow, including
  the warning that loose magnets jump to the print head.
- **Limits that matter:** desktop FDM holds roughly ±0.5% (floor ±0.5 mm), desktop resin ±0.5%
  (floor ±0.10 mm). Resin needs 0.4 mm supported walls, so a printed shell over a US quarter comes
  out about 0.8 mm oversize and 20 times too light. Metal coin shells remain a machining job.
  Printing wins on objects that are already moulded plastic.

# Report 07-smart-materials-and-actuators.md


## Summary (07)
- **The valuable use of this slice is not "a material that visibly moves" but "a hold, latch, clutch or bond with no motor, no noise and no standby power".** The practical toolbox is now: nitinol wire (silent pull), electropermanent magnets (pulse on, pulse off, zero holding power), programmable magnet sheets (selective sticking), fuses made of meltable, soluble or subliming solids (delays and self-erasing gimmicks), jamming (two stiffness states in one limp object), and electrostatic clutches (thin, silent, milliwatts).
- **Nitinol wire is the single most useful part.** Dynalloy's own table: a 0.10 mm wire pulls 143 g, needs about 200 mA, contracts in about 1 s, re-extends in about 1 s, and is rated for millions of cycles at 3 to 5% stroke. It is a hair-thin, silent "muscle" that fits inside a 1 mm cavity. Magic has used nitinol almost only as a visible self-bending spoon.
- **Electropermanent magnets (EPMs) fix the three weaknesses of magnets in magic**: a permanent magnet is always on (found by any steel object or phone compass), an electromagnet hums, heats and drains batteries. An EPM holds with zero power, switches with a pulse of tens of millijoules, and in the off state leaks almost nothing. A commercial drone unit holds 200 N. The mechanism also ships inside a Logitech MX Master 3 mouse.
- **Magnetism can now be a code, not just a force.** MIT's Mixels (UIST 2022) writes 3 mm north/south pixels onto $0.008/cm2 fridge-magnet sheet with a $62 printhead; two 25 mm squares stick with 1.09 N only to their coded partner. That is a new method primitive: objects that selectively attract one specific other object out of many.
- **Gadolinium is ferromagnetic below about 20 C and not above.** A chilled slug sticks to a magnet; warmed in a hand it lets go and afterwards passes any magnet test. This is a hold that releases on hand heat with no electronics and erases its own evidence. I found no magic use (unverified).
- **Material fuses give delays with nobody present and nothing left behind**: cyclododecane (a waxy solid used by art conservators) sublimes away in days to weeks with no residue; gallium melts at 29.76 C; polycaprolactone softens at 60 C; cold-water PVA dissolves; an electrically debonding adhesive (in the iPhone 16) releases with 9 to 30 V in 5 s to 6 min and zero force.
- **Jamming gives one object two mechanical states that look identical.** A stack of n thin sheets in an evacuated sleeve is up to n squared times stiffer than the same stack unevacuated (theory; 10 layers gives up to 100x). A hand-held syringe is enough vacuum. This removes the rigid insert that classic "stiff rope" and "stiff handkerchief" methods must hide or steal away.
- **Textile electrostatic clutches (EPFL, 2019 to 2022) hold 20 N/cm2 at 300 V, weigh 30 mg/cm2, switch in under 15 ms and draw 1 mW/cm2.** That is a lock thinner than a playing card. Nothing like it exists in magic.
- **Threads: the real gain is in what a thread can also do.** A single 12 um Kevlar filament holds about 35 g (calc), a 17 um UHMWPE filament about 75 g (calc), versus about 9 g (calc) for a 12 um nylon strand. A 10 um tungsten wire holds about 24 g (calc) and conducts current, so a thread can power, signal or cut itself free.
- **Weight is a cheap, strong, non-electronic anomaly.** A one-inch tungsten cube is 300 g (supplier figure); the same cube in aluminium is 44 g and in printed PLA about 20 g (calc). Identical-looking objects can differ 15-fold in weight.
- **Overhyped for live use:** HASEL and dielectric elastomers (10 to 22 kV), liquid crystal elastomers and azobenzene films (lab chemistry), self-healing polymers as "cut and restore" (hours, soft materials), electrowetting droplet tables, ferrofluid, aerogel, mu-metal as a "magnet blocker", ionic polymer actuators.

# Report 08-chemistry-inks-biology.md


## Summary (08)
- **The most useful single item in this slice is sold at every stationer.** Pilot FriXion ink is
  a bistable mark: it goes colourless at about 60 to 65 C and comes back only at about -10 to
  -20 C, so at room temperature it remembers whichever state it was last put in. A salt-ice
  bucket (-22 C), a domestic freezer or freezer spray is a hands-off "developer". Magic has used
  it only for heat vanishes.
- **Thermal receipt paper is a gimmickless secret-writing surface.** It marks from fingernail
  friction alone, so a till receipt, parking ticket or shipping label can be nail-written with no
  nail writer. The object is ordinary and plausibly borrowed.
- **Chemistry gives cheap "delay" and "erase the evidence" primitives that electronics cannot.**
  Conservators' cyclododecane holds parts and then sublimes away in days to weeks with no residue;
  menthol and camphor do the same faster; PVA film and string dissolve in water; cocoa butter
  releases at 34 to 38 C and gallium at 29.8 C; dissolving sweets have been used as real-world
  delay fuses. None of these leave a mechanism to find.
- **Delayed-appearance marks exist for skin, paper and banknotes.** Dihydroxyacetone (self-tanner)
  is applied clear and develops in 2 to 4 hours, lasting 3 to 7 days. Iron-gall ink writes pale
  and darkens in air. A starch mark on a cotton banknote is invisible until an ordinary
  counterfeit-detector pen is swiped over it.
- **Trigger-specific latent images are off the shelf:** body heat (31 C leuco pigment, about $5
  per 10 g), water or condensation (hydrochromic white ink, about GBP 20 per 100 ml), daylight
  (photochromic pigment, cyanotype paper), 365 nm UV (inkjet-loadable invisible inks), rubbing
  (thermal paper, carbonless paper, scratch-and-sniff), and cold.
- **Self-erasing information is practical at three timescales:** seconds to minutes (photochromic
  fade, phosphor afterglow, thymolphthalein ink), on demand (FriXion plus any 60 C source), and
  days (research rewritable papers, 3 to 5 days, not purchasable).
- **Taste is an unused covert channel.** Miraculin (sour reads as sweet for 30 minutes to 2 hours)
  and gymnemic acid (sugar reads as nothing for 10+ minutes) change one person's perception with
  nothing for anyone else to see. They can also carry a cue to one spectator.
- **Research chromics are mostly dead ends for a hobbyist.** MIT's PhotoChromeleon/ChromoUpdate
  needs a lab UV projector and 1 to 30+ minutes per image; rewritable paper, perovskite and
  time-gated security inks are not sold; up-conversion inks need an invisible 980 nm laser.
- **Biology is slow, dim or microscopic, with two exceptions.** Mimosa pudica and Venus flytraps
  can be fired electrically (about 1.3 to 1.5 V, a few microcoulombs) through hidden electrodes,
  and dinoflagellates flash when shaken. Slime moulds, glowing petunias and magnetotactic bacteria
  do not work live.
- **Hazard and transport limits decide what is tourable.** Nitrocellulose is a regulated explosive
  or flammable solid; water beads now have a US federal safety standard; cobalt chloride, the
  mercury-based Old Nassau clock and phosphorus "smoke from fingertips" should simply be dropped.

# Report 09-levitation-fields-fluids.md


## Summary (09)
- **True levitation is a poor fit for live magic; hidden millinewtons are a very good fit.** Every
  real levitation method either lifts almost nothing (acoustic: milligrams to 0.6 g), has a gap of
  1 to 3 cm above an obvious base (magnetic), or needs a chamber, cryogen or drone. Audiences also
  guess "magnets" or "it's a science thing" at once. The same physics used covertly is far
  stronger, because the force only has to be big enough to tip a decision.
- **The key design rule is: apply a small force where the system is unstable or resonant.** A
  pendulum with a quality factor of 100 to 300 turns a 0.1 mN periodic push into a swing of tens of
  degrees (my estimate). A coin on edge, a ball on a divider, a spinner about to stop and a die on
  an edge all need microjoules to decide. This is what makes cheap coils, tiny air jets and
  ultrasound useful.
- **Eddy currents give a force that passes the magnet test.** Braking force on a moving conductor
  is proportional to speed and is exactly zero at rest
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddy_current_brake)). A solid aluminium or copper
  spinner, top or rolling coin can be slowed from under the table, yet a spectator's magnet does
  nothing to it. I found no magic product built on this (unverified absence).
- **A switchable field under a table now costs a few dollars and no moving parts**: coils, an
  H-bridge, a Hall sensor and a microcontroller. Robert-Houdin's Light and Heavy Chest (Palais
  Royal programme, from 1845) was the same idea with a single on/off electromagnet
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Eug%C3%A8ne_Robert-Houdin)). What is new is
  closed-loop control, many coils, sensing through the surface, and battery operation.
- **Computer-controlled thread is the most practical "levitation" upgrade.** Cable-driven parallel
  robots are mature and open (Skycam, Hangprinter; ART+COM hung 714 spheres on thin steel wires in
  2008). A torque-controlled brushless winch (SimpleFOC) can hold a thread at a gram of tension.
  Unloading the prop (helium, magnet lift) lets the thread be thinner still.
- **Acoustic levitation: honest numbers.** TinyLev lifts beads up to about 4 mm at densities up to
  2.2 g/cm3 on 10 W and about 20 V, and a kit was reported at US$70. Phased arrays (SonicSurface,
  256 emitters) are open hardware. Sound fields can now be computed around obstacles at more than
  10,000 updates per second. The disguise problem (a tile of 256 silver cans) is partly solved by
  acoustically transparent fabric.
- **Silent hidden air is now possible.** Piezo microblowers (0.7 L/min, 1.4 kPa, driven at 24 to
  27 kHz, sold as "silent") and corona ion-wind jets have no audible motor. They give roughly 0.3
  to 10 mN (my estimate), enough for flames, smoke, tissue, balanced cards and pinwheels.
- **Electrostatics passes through glass and needs microwatts**, so a charged performer or a hidden
  electrode can move straws, foil, bubbles and thin water streams inside a sealed glass. A
  body-charging gimmick already exists in magic (Yigal Mesika's Electric Touch, unverified page).
- **Superconductors, photophoresis, ion-drive craft, induction levitation of aluminium and optical
  tweezers are dead ends for a hobbyist performing live** (logistics, vacuum, scale or power).
- **Vibrated-fluid phenomena** (walking droplets, a boat floating under a levitated liquid layer,
  Leidenfrost ratchets, the Kaye effect) are cheap and astonishing but are installations or video
  pieces, not hidden methods.

# Report 10-sensing-and-covert-communication.md


## Summary (10)
- **The market already sells "read one thing, buzz the performer".** RFID decks read at 11 cm (Insight Pro, $550), magnet detectors at 12 to 18 cm (Sixth Sense 3, $300), electronic impression pads ($999 for ThoughtCast Pro), smart dice and vibrating receivers all exist [11][15][53][47]. Rebuilding those is cheap but not new. The gap is elsewhere.
- **The real opening is complete, continuous state knowledge of every prop**, held by a controller rather than the performer's head. With the full state known, forces, controls, stooges and pre-show become unnecessary, and the spectator's choice can be truly free.
- **Fully passive sensing is now cheap**: the object the audience handles can contain nothing at all. A $6 24-bit load-cell ADC [27], a 4-channel 28-bit inductive converter that ignores magnets [26], a $75 thermal array [44], a $27 60 GHz radar [30] and a $19 camera-plus-microphone board the size of a postage stamp [56] all sit in the table, the room or the performer's clothing.
- **Battery-free tags became sensors, not just IDs.** IDSense (Disney Research, CHI 2015) classifies touch and movement of 20 objects at about 93% from changes in the RFID radio link alone [10]. A hobby UHF reader reads up to 300 tags per second at up to 4.9 m [9]. Tags now come as small as 1.2 x 1.2 x 0.55 mm [1].
- **Magnet sensing moved from "is a magnet near" to "where is it and which way is it facing".** MagX tracks passive magnets in 5 degrees of freedom with 8 magnetometers, about 0.76 cm error at 11 cm [20][22]. This is the genuinely new thing relative to existing "which hand" devices.
- **Writing capture through paper is mature.** Wacom's EMR smartpads read a pen through about 80 sheets (8 mm) [48]. Recognising writing from pen motion alone (IMU pens) works in research but is weak across writers [54]; restrict it to small symbol sets.
- **Reading people is mostly a dead end as a method, and useful as theatre.** Voice stress analysis performed at chance in a US field study [62]; micro-expressions are rare and unreliable [63]; pupil dilation gives an AUC of about 0.70 on a single trial [58]; a consumer EEG headband needs averaged trials and reduced guessing entropy by only 15 to 40% in the best-known study [59][60]. None of these can carry an effect alone.
- **What can be read from people reliably is coarse and behavioural**: where heads are pointing (real-time head pose for a whole room on a phone [73]), presence and breathing by radar [30], and what was said aloud (on-device Whisper in about 273 MB of memory with half-second updates [67]). Head pose opens a new architecture: secret moves timed by measured attention.
- **Output channels are no longer the bottleneck.** Display glasses that let a presenter keep eye contact are a retail product [71][69], and an EMG wristband for silent input ships with one of them at $799 [69]. Contact-lens displays do not exist as products [72].
- **Radio is the main reliability risk in a room full of phones.** Prefer links that are quiet until needed (NFC, IR, wired), or connectionless short frames on a fixed channel (ESP-NOW, 250-byte frames [74]), and design every routine so that a missing packet leads to an "out", not a failure.

# Report 11-hci-haptics-and-body-directed-tech.md


## Summary (11)
- **The strongest transfer in this slice is the private channel, and the cheapest version is not
  ultrasonic.** A $9 bone-conduction transducer under a table sends audio through a person's
  elbows when they block their own ears (Adafruit's own product page describes the elbow-bone
  route). The act of "proving you cannot hear" becomes the listening method.
- **Parametric (ultrasonic) directional speakers are now a $50 to $150 hobby build** (a 95-element
  open design is on GitHub), but cheap ones sound thin, leak by reflection in small rooms, and run
  the ultrasound carrier well above the 110 dB occupational guideline. They are a parlor tool for
  short bursts, not a close-up tool.
- **Mid-air ultrasonic touch is real but weak and fussy.** A 16x16 array gives an 8.6 mm pressure
  point over roughly 5 to 70 cm in a ±30° cone. It needs bare palm skin, clothing absorbs it, it
  buzzes audibly, and the focus sits around 145 to 155 dB. A loudspeaker-driven air-vortex cannon
  gives a clearer "breath" at 1 m for a tenth of the cost and has a perfect disguise (a ported
  speaker).
- **Electrical muscle stimulation cannot secretly choose for a spectator.** The agency results are
  specific: stimulation 160 ms after a cue speeds a reaction by 80 ms and still feels self-caused,
  but only for a movement the person was already about to make. Fired earlier, people say "it
  moves before I do". EMS is an overt, consent-based "puppet" effect, not a covert force.
- **Body steering without electrodes on a limb exists**: galvanic vestibular stimulation (1 mA
  behind the ears moved untrained standers about 4 cm toward the anode), the hanger reflex (skin
  shear on the head, waist or wrist), and 80 Hz tendon vibration (illusory arm movement in 12 of
  16 people, eyes closed). All need a worn device and informed consent.
- **Held-object feel is controllable without changing true mass**: moving an internal weight
  changes perceived heft and length (Shifty, Transcalibur); asymmetric vibration produces a felt
  pull (Traxion, Grabity). A box that weighs the same on a scale can be made to "feel heavier" on
  demand. This turns spectator examination into a channel the performer controls.
- **Chemistry beats electronics for skin and tongue.** 10% menthol feels cold within about 10 s;
  capsaicin warmth builds over minutes (a usable delay); miraculin turns sour to sweet for up to
  30 minutes; atomised eucalyptol makes inhaled air feel colder with no cooling hardware. None of
  it may be applied covertly.
- **Flat PCB coil arrays are a general secret mover.** Reactile's 4-layer boards (10 mm grid,
  about $80 per 16x40 cm board) move small magnets silently with no moving parts. Built into a
  close-up pad this replaces threads, reels and under-table gantries for small objects.
- **On-skin and battery-free output is mature at craft level**: sub-35 µm electro-tactile tattoos,
  silent shape-memory "Springlets" skin stickers, and NFC-powered e-ink that holds its image with
  no battery. These are mainly performer-cueing and "printed mark that changes" primitives.
- **Prior use in magic is thin.** The classic precedents are Robert-Houdin's Light and Heavy Chest
  (the 1856 Algeria version added an electric shock through the handle) and sleight-based "PK
  touches".
  I found no documented performing use of phased-array haptics, coil-array pads, or chemical
  taste modulators as method; parametric speakers have long been joked about for "making someone
  hear voices" but I found no marketed magic product (search was limited).

# Report 12-software-ai-computation.md


## Summary (12)
- **The highest-value use of computation is before the show, not during it.** Search,
  optimisation and generation can produce stacks, forcing structures, book texts, anagram trees and
  thousands of indexed physical outs that no person would design by hand. At performance time
  there is no electronics at all, so the "a computer did it" theory has nothing to attach to.
  Williams and McOwan showed the pattern in 2014 (a simulated-annealing deck stack that beat
  Aronson's Simon-Eyes on average questions needed, 3.85 against 4.04); almost nobody has repeated
  it with 2026 solvers, LLMs and print-on-demand.
- **Diffusion "illusion" images are best understood as an outs compressor, not as a spectacle.**
  One printed, examinable object can now carry 2 to 4 full-quality pictures selected by rotation,
  viewing distance, colour of light, a second transparency, or re-assembly of jigsaw pieces. Code
  is MIT-licensed and runs on free-tier Colab (Visual Anagrams) or Colab notebooks (Diffusion
  Illusions).
- **Offline speech recognition is now small enough to hide in a prop and fast enough to act
  inside a sentence.** Vendor benchmarks put Moonshine streaming models at about 237 ms (tiny) to
  802 ms (medium) on a Raspberry Pi 5; an ESP32-S3 with Espressif's MultiNet recognises up to 300
  fixed phrases offline with no training. The performer never has to peek, pump or type.
- **For closed sets (52 cards, numbers, times) do not put an LLM in the loop.** A constrained
  vocabulary recogniser is faster and more reliable. LLMs and embedding models earn their place
  for open-ended answers: mapping "anything you can think of" to the nearest of several hundred
  prepared physical outs.
- **Batteryless NFC e-paper is the cleanest dynamic "print".** A 296 x 128 panel refreshes in about
  2 s powered only by the NFC field and is afterwards inert: no battery, no radio. It is thick as
  sold (12.4 mm board outline) and reads as e-ink at close range.
- **Ink, sand, flaps, clock hands and burn marks beat pixels.** A thermal printer costs about $50
  and prints 50 to 80 mm/s; a servo plotter costs about EUR 15; pen plotters make real ballpoint
  "handwriting" overnight. These are all much stronger as pre-show out factories than as live
  reveal machines, because live they are noisy and slow.
- **Human "free choices" are predictable to a useful, stated degree.** A 5-gram predictor of
  binary choices is right about 70% of the time; Google's Quick, Draw! set holds 50 million
  timed drawings in 345 categories. Confidence-gated commitment (only commit when the model is
  sure) is an unexploited architecture.
- **Cryptographic "proof I predicted this" can be faked with multiple outs.** Twelve PDFs sharing
  one MD5 hash were published as a mock election prediction in 2007; today colliding PDF or image
  pairs take seconds. This is theatre for a technical audience and must be framed as such.
- **Hobbyist dynamic projection mapping is still an order of magnitude slower than the lab.**
  DynaFlash runs at 1,000 fps with 3 ms delay; a consumer pipeline is 30 to 50 ms, which is 9 to
  15 mm of smear on a prop moving at 0.3 m/s. Use static or slow targets.
- **Real-time deepfakes, voice cloning, live image generation and AI songs work but are L1.**
  They read as "AI", need a screen or speaker, and raise consent problems. Keep them brief.

# Report 13-on-demand-fabrication-and-llm-cad.md


## Summary (13)
- **The first unit now costs about the same as the hundredth for most flat and printed things.**
  Five 2-layer boards cost $2 at JLCPCB with a 24-hour build; five flex boards cost the same
  entry price; one custom 55-card deck costs $16.95; a 300-page paperback costs $4.60 to print;
  a single CNC part starts at $5 with a minimum order of one. Setup fees have collapsed, which
  is what makes one-off, personalised and disposable props affordable.
- **Brute-force variety is cheap.** MakePlayingCards prints up to 612 different cards in one
  order for $139, about $0.23 per unique card. Every outcome of a routine can be pre-made as a
  physical object instead of being forced or switched in.
- **A circuit cannot hide inside the thickness of one playing card, but it gets close.**
  JLCPCB flex is 0.07 mm (1 layer) or 0.11 mm (2 layer) finished, and a transparent PET flex is
  0.14 mm. The limit is components: the thinnest common LEDs are about 0.25 mm, so a lit card is
  about 0.5 to 0.6 mm locally against roughly 0.3 mm for a real card. Passive copper (coils,
  antennas, capacitive pads, shims) fits inside a card with room to spare.
- **Thin rigid boards are a standard catalogue option.** FR4 at 0.4 and 0.6 mm, castellated
  modules down to 10 x 10 mm, boards as small as 3 x 3 mm, 4/4 mil traces, and matte black,
  white or clear-over-black finishes that pass as cosmetic parts.
- **Metal is the weak point for services, and coins are a legal problem.** Printed steel is
  $0.21/g and cast silver has 0.6 mm walls with 0.3 mm detail, but neither gives a struck-coin
  surface. US law (18 U.S.C. 489) prohibits making discs "in the likeness or similitude" of
  coins, which is why professional gimmick makers machine genuine coins. Services can make the
  inserts, rings, slugs and brass props around the coin, not the coin.
- **Turnaround from services is 3 to 10 days door to door; "tonight for tomorrow" needs
  in-house machines.** The set that covers most of it: a multi-material FDM printer, a 12K to
  16K resin printer, a craft cutter, and either a small laser or a UV flatbed printer.
- **A consumer UV flatbed printer (eufyMake E1, from $1,799 on sale) prints on arbitrary
  objects** up to 330 x 420 mm with white ink and up to 5 mm of raised texture. This is the
  single machine that most changes "prove ordinariness": printing on genuine cards, real
  product packaging, borrowed-looking objects.
- **Looking factory-made is cheap; looking factory-sealed is cheap too.** Short-run retail
  boxes ("tiny minimums", 7 to 15 business days), holographic stickers (50 minimum, 4 days),
  shrink bags and an impulse sealer cost tens of dollars. Magic makers use almost none of this.

# Report 14-electronics-building-blocks.md


## Summary (14)
- **The battery, not the chip, sets the size of every gimmick.** A complete Bluetooth computer is
  now 2.45 x 2.25 x 0.42 mm (nRF54L15 CSP [V]) or 1.7 x 2.0 x 0.33 mm (DA14531 WLCSP, $0.81 [V]);
  a general MCU is 1.6 x 0.86 mm (TI MSPM0C1104 [V]). The thinnest stocked LiPo is 0.4 mm but
  22 x 55 mm for 25 mAh at $15 [V]. Design the power first.
- **The most useful move is to take the battery out of the handled object entirely.** Three
  proven ways: 13.56 MHz NFC harvesting (15 mW from NTAG I2C plus [V], "up to 30 mW" from NTAG 5
  boost [V], a few mA from ST25DV [S]); cheap loosely coupled induction (Adafruit 5 V coil kit:
  LEDs light 0 to 80 mm above a 70 mm, 0.5 mm thick coil, $14.95 with ten receivers [V]); and Qi
  (40 x 29 mm receiver, 5 V 500 mA, contact range only [V]).
- **Standby is solved.** An AirTag idles at 2.3 µA, about 11 years on a CR2032 [V]. A 45 nA RTC
  (RV-3028-C7, 3.2 x 1.5 x 0.8 mm [V]) or a 35 nA power-gating timer (TPL5111 [V]) plus a 0.54 µA
  hall switch in a 1.4 x 1.1 mm package (DRV5032 [V]) lets a prop sleep for years on a 12 mm coin
  cell and act once at a set time or on a magnet pass.
- **Logic is nearly free, so it can be thrown away.** Padauk PMS150C is $0.033 [V], CH32V003 is
  $0.14 to $0.28 on LCSC [S], an addressable RGB LED 1.1 x 1.1 x 0.33 mm is $0.05 to $0.09 [S].
  A burnable or bin-able "sense and signal once" unit is well under $1 in parts; the coin cell is
  the largest cost and the only real disposal hazard.
- **Credit-card thickness (0.76 mm) holds a full computer; playing-card thickness (0.3 mm) holds
  only a passive tag.** Two-layer flex is 0.11 mm [V], wafer-level chips are 0.33 to 0.42 mm,
  the thinnest cells 0.4 to 0.5 mm, a flexible e-paper panel 0.3 mm [V]. A "thick card" (two
  cards laminated, about 0.6 mm) is the realistic smart playing card.
- **Silent actuation exists but is weak; strong actuation is loud.** Electromagnet release,
  shape-memory wire, flex-PCB coils and piezo pumps (Bartels BP7 under 50 dB, 30 x 15 x 3.8 mm,
  2 g [V]) are quiet. Solenoids (80 g at 3 mm, 1.1 A, audible click [V]), geared motors and
  servos are not. Rank by noise before force when designing for close-up.
- **RF does not come out of a solid metal coin or ring; magnetism does.** Skin depth in brass is
  about 0.035 mm at 13.56 MHz and microns at 2.4 GHz, but about 4 mm at 1 kHz (my calculation,
  section 2.6). Metal props should talk with magnets, hall sensors and low-frequency coils, or
  have a non-metal window, as AirTag and Oura do.
- **Existence proofs are everywhere and mostly open.** Pixels dice put an nRF52, LEDs, 60 mAh
  cell, 10 mm charge coil and accelerometer on a folded flex in a 16 mm die [V]; the Oura Ring 5
  has a 10.2 to 10.5 mAh cell on a flex in titanium [S]; a $20 Colmi R02 ring is a ready-made
  accelerometer-plus-BLE finger controller with an open protocol [V]; Chipolo Card is a 2.4 mm
  tracker with a two-year cell [V].
- **Good prototyping paths:** Seeed XIAO boards (21 x 17.8 mm; ESP32-C6 $5.20 [V], nRF54L15
  $9.90 [S], ESP32-S3 Sense with camera and mic $18.90 [V]), then shrink to an 8 x 8 x 1 mm SiP
  (Insight SiP ISP1507 [V]) or 7.8 x 8.8 x 1.3 mm module (Fanstel BC832, $5.74 at 1k [V]), then
  to bare WLCSP on flex only if needed.
- **Best new architectures from this slice:** batteryless sealed props powered from the mat;
  time-delayed props that act days after the performer has left; disposable instrumented
  environment objects; and one-shot hold-and-release pellets under half a gram.

# Report 16-chance-choice-and-control.md


## Summary (16)
- **Randomisers are deterministic machines whose trustworthiness comes from sensitivity, not
  from physics.** A spring coin-flipper lands the starting side up 100% of the time (Diaconis,
  Holmes & Montgomery 2007). Human flips are 50.8% same-side over 350,757 flips (Bartoš et al.),
  which is useless on stage, but a penny *spun* on its edge can land tails about 80% of the time
  and a coin with an invisibly shaved edge "always" lands one way. The useful design rule: pick
  the randomiser whose mechanism has the fewest bounces and the lowest energy, because that is
  where small hidden inputs dominate.
- **The size of bias needed is surprisingly small and usually below perception.** A roulette
  wheel tilted 0.2° already creates a large arc of the rim from which the ball never drops (Small
  & Tse 2012, confirming Thorp). 0.2° is 1 mm of rise across a 300 mm table and moves a freely
  rolling ball about 5 cm in 2 s (my calculation). Ordinary dice whose 1-6 axis is 0.1 to 0.2 mm
  short on 16 mm show a statistically certain bias (Labby 2009, 315,672 die-rolls), but that bias
  is under 0.3 percentage points: geometric and mass biases need to be large (centre of mass
  moved about 40% of the half-width) before one face reaches even 40%.
- **So passive bias is a weak tool and closed-loop control is the strong one.** A servo-shifted
  weight in a 3D-printed die reaches only about 40% on the target face (James Stanley, 2021). By
  contrast, hobbyists have closed loops around thrown objects with 200 to 600 ms of flight (Mark
  Rober's dartboard, Shane Wighton's hoop), and the Ishikawa lab's janken robot wins 100% of
  rock-paper-scissors rounds by recognising the hand in 1 ms. Magic has barely used "sense the
  honest randomiser late and nudge once".
- **The cheapest certainty is to replace the randomiser's output, not bias its motion.** A $200
  hobbyist card shuffler with a Pi Zero camera and bins (3DprintedLife) reads every card and can
  deal any planned order while looking like a randomiser; a commercial casino shuffler already
  contains a camera that knows the full deck order (IOActive 2023).
- **Human "free" choices are heavily concentrated and the numbers are published.** "Name a
  card": Ace of Spades 24.6%, Queen of Hearts 13.7% (Olson, Amlani & Rensink 2012, n=423 valid).
  "First digit that comes to mind": 7 at 28.4% (Kubovy & Psotka 1976, pooled n=1,770). Four cards
  in a row: 52 to 60% take the third from the left (Pailhès & Kuhn). Four shelf rows: 71% take
  the middle two (Christenfeld 1995).
- **Outs are an engineering variable.** Using Olson's table, k outs covering the top-k named
  cards give 24.6% (k=1), 38.5% (2), 50.6% (4), 63.9% (8), 72.2% (12), 84.4% (20), 90.2% (26)
  (my calculation). Asking several people multiplies it: the chance that at least one of 10
  people names the Ace of Spades is 94%.
- **People cannot generate random sequences, and a trivial model exploits it.** Aaronson's
  n-gram key-press predictor is right about 70% of the time (70 to 80% in his class); at 70% per
  round a best-of-15 game is won 95% of the time (my calculation). Rock-paper-scissors play shows
  win-stay and lose-shift conditional responses (Wang, Xu & Zhou 2014, 360 players, 300 rounds).
- **Mathematical procedures can be tuned from "likely" to "near certain".** The Kruskal count
  succeeds 85.4% with one deck, face cards = 5, performer starting on card 1 (Lagarias, Rains &
  Vanderbei; reproduced). Counting the letters of each card's name instead gives 95.8%; two decks
  with face = 5 give 97.9%; two decks with letter-counting give 99.8% (my simulation, 100,000 to
  200,000 trials). A computer-searched text or stack can make it exactly 100%.
- **Priming is the weak member of the family.** The best-documented "conversational prime"
  force reaches 17.8% for the exact card (Pailhès & Kuhn 2020, n=90). It is an out-multiplier,
  not a method.
- **Whole-audience statistics plus one-off printing is an under-used architecture.** With 256
  seats each holding a different printed chain of predictions, 8 people are certain to hold a
  perfect record after 5 honest coin flips and 1 after 8 (the tipster scheme as theatre; Derren
  Brown's "The System" used 6^5 = 7,776 people). This must be framed honestly.

# Report 17-time-evidence-and-examination.md


## Summary (17)
- **A bond that ends itself with no residue is now a catalogue item.** Art conservators' volatile
  binding media (cyclododecane, menthol, camphene) are waxy solids that hold, then sublime away.
  Measured lifetimes run from about 1 day (camphene) to about 12 days (menthol or cyclododecane,
  1 to 2 mm blob at 20 C) to months (cyclododecanol). Magic has, as far as I can find, never used
  them.
- **Structural adhesive that lets go on a low-voltage signal is in every iPhone 16.** Electrically
  debonding tapes and epoxies hold at structural strength and release in about 90 s at 9 V, or in
  about 10 s at 45 V. No heat, no sound, no moving latch. The limit is that both bonded faces must
  be conductive.
- **Electronics-free timers with published precision exist in the diagnostics literature.** Sugar
  or wax barriers in paper strips give delays of 44 s to 53 min (variation 11 to 24 percent) or
  1 min to 2 h (about plus or minus 6 percent), and several delays can run in sequence from one
  wetting.
- **Timed corroding links are a $4 fishing-tackle part.** Galvanic timed releases are rated from
  1 day to 7 days and beyond, but only in salt water, and the time shifts about 25 percent for a
  3 C change in temperature.
- **Water-soluble structure is cheap and comes in every form**: film (carp-fishing PVA bags,
  about $0.20 each, gone in 30 s to 3 min), sewing thread, paper (30 s or less), and 3D-printable
  filament (hours). Gelatin is a temperature gate: it swells but does not dissolve below 30 C.
- **Energy and the trigger can be sent into a sealed, battery-free object**: 5 to 10 W through
  12 to 30 mm of tabletop with extended-range Qi, or about 10 to 20 mW from an NFC field, which a
  spectator's own phone can supply. That makes "they triggered it themselves, weeks later"
  technically reachable.
- **Examination is far weaker evidence than audiences believe.** In the Los Alamos seal studies,
  244 seal designs were all defeated, median 43 s, by one person with low-tech tools; volunteers
  comparing a seal impression against a reference were wrong nearly a third of the time; and
  tamper-detection specialists did no better than chance on prepared consumer packages. The
  transferable principles: inspectors have no reference exemplar, they check the feature they are
  pointed at, and a seal's presence is taken as proof.
- **Delay has a perceptual cost as well as a methodological benefit.** Lengthening the gap between
  a false transfer and the reveal from 1 s to 32 s cut the rated strength of the magic by 38
  percent on average. Long delays need a frame that keeps the "before" state alive (a signature, a
  photo, a seal the spectator applied).
- **Identity marks can be machine-duplicated in seconds**, on props: a pen plotter replays captured
  strokes ($430 to $700), a handheld inkjet prints on almost any surface ($110 to $200 at launch),
  and a pocket laser engraves a small mark in about 10 s ($259 and up).
- **Most "self-destructing electronics" are still lab-only.** Dissolvable silicon, depolymerising
  substrates and shattering glass chips are not purchasable. The realistic hobby form of a
  vanishing gimmick is mechanical: sublimable wax, PVA, ice, flash paper, burn-through thread.

# Report 18-unprepared-and-borrowed-objects.md


## Summary (18)
- **An ungimmicked object already carries a serial number; nobody has been reading it.** Paper, card
  stock, print and metal surfaces are unique at the 1 to 100 micron scale, and this has been readable
  with a commodity scanner since 2009 and with a phone camera since about 2015 to 2017 (Clarkson 2009;
  Toreini 2017, 0% false accept / 0% false reject on their datasets from one photo; Wong and Wu 2015 to
  2017). This turns "mark the object" into "enrol the object", which leaves nothing to find.
- **A shop-fresh deck is probably machine-distinguishable from the backs after one enrolment pass.** No
  paper on this was found (a real gap), but the ingredients are established: card makers accept back
  registration errors up to 1/32 inch (0.8 mm), which is the whole basis of casino edge sorting, and
  print-plus-paper microstructure fingerprints work on offset-printed stock. This is the most valuable
  open experiment in this slice and costs nothing but a flatbed scan.
- **Hobby metal-detector physics moves into the table.** Phase-sensitive eddy-current sensing (vending
  acceptors at 1 to 3 mm; TI LDC1612-class chips at up to about one coil diameter; hobby VLF detectors
  with target-ID at 15 cm and more; the two range figures are unverified) can tell that a borrowed coin or ring is present, roughly where, and
  which alloy family it is, through a tabletop or a closed fist. Existing magic "which hand" products
  all require a magnetic coin; the non-magnetic version appears to be unbuilt.
- **Currency metallurgy gives free information and free actuation.** UK 1p/2p since 1992 and 5p/10p
  since 2012 are plated steel; euro 1/2/5 cent are copper-plated steel; the centres of 1 and 2 euro
  coins are magnetic; many modern Canadian coins are steel. US notes carry enough iron-oxide ink to
  swing toward a neodymium magnet. The alloy changes also encode the date range, so an inductive or
  weight reading can "divine" that a penny is pre-1982 or pre-1992 without seeing it.
- **Non-magnetic metal can be pushed, dragged and spun without contact** by a rotating or travelling
  magnetic field (induction-motor principle). Pham et al. (Nature 2021) showed full 6-DOF manipulation
  of a copper sphere with rotating dipole fields. My estimate is that a flat copper-cored coin on a
  table will spin or creep above a 3,000 rpm two-pole neodymium rotor at 10 to 20 mm gap, with
  milliwatt heating; cupronickel, platinum, titanium and tungsten objects respond 5 to 15 times less.
- **Vibration is the universal actuator for non-metal objects.** A plate oscillated horizontally with
  an asymmetric waveform moves anything resting on it (Reznik and Canny's Universal Planar
  Manipulator; Vose, Umbanhowar and Lynch's programmable velocity fields; Zhou et al.'s Chladni-plate
  steering). The acceleration needed to break friction corresponds to only about 20 microns of travel
  at 60 Hz (own estimate), which is invisible; the giveaway is hum and ripples in drinks.
- **People now carry magnets and radios, so some "gimmicked" methods work on borrowed things.** A
  MagSafe phone, an earbud case and a steel bottle cap are all detectable by the magnet sensors magic
  already sells. Phones leak presence passively over BLE. The ethical line is clear and is stated in
  the catalog: presence and position are fine with consent; names, identifiers and payment data are
  not.
- **Coins of the same denomination have individual voices.** A 2022 coin-validation paper measured
  thirty genuine coins of one type ringing anywhere from 16.92 to 17.45 kHz while one coin repeats to
  about 12 Hz. That is an unmarked identity readable by any microphone.
- **Thermal, acoustic and weight side channels identify "the one they touched".** Residual heat is
  readable for 30 to 60 s on plastic and longer on coins; a tap or a set-down has an object-specific
  sound (Knocker: 98% in the lab on 23 objects); a four-corner load-cell table knows the weight and
  position of everything on it.
- **A few quiet effectors act on paper, liquids and eyewear:** kilovolt electrodes (tissue lifts at
  roughly 2 kV/cm, own estimate), silent piezo blowers, breath-figure latent images on glass,
  a thermal print head acting on the spectator's own till receipt, and UV on photochromic lenses
  (15 to 60 s to darken). Each has a hazard or a tell that is listed below.
- **Overhyped for live use:** diamagnetic forces on water or fruit, acoustic levitation of borrowed
  objects, anything requiring a web page on the spectator's phone to read a sensor, and any reading of
  contactless bank cards (illegal and pointless).

# Report 19-hidden-microfactories-and-brute-force-outs.md


## Summary (19)
- **Magic has adopted exactly one micro-factory: the motorised Bluetooth thermal printer.** Dealer
  products (PITATA Smart Printer and Smart Printer Mini at about $400, 76 x 42 x 25 mm; BlackBox
  "Cosmos" printer wallet at $450; Electricks support for the consumer PeriPage printer) all hide a
  stock receipt mechanism and print a slip on app command. The engine inside is a $15 to $20
  "cat printer" whose BLE protocol has been reverse-engineered several times. Everything else in
  this report (pen, laser, inkjet, electro-etch, UV mask, heat matrix, pressure) appears to be
  unused in dealer products.
- **The most useful reframing is "write-through".** The factory does not have to be inside the
  sealed thing. Pressure crosses a paper envelope (carbonless paper), light crosses glass and
  clear plastic (laser or UV onto thermal or photochromic paper), near-infrared crosses
  visually opaque "IR-pass" plastic, heat crosses a thin mirror or film, magnetism crosses any
  non-ferrous wall. Each gives "it was sealed and signed before you chose, and nothing was opened".
- **Print during the reveal motion, not before it.** Swipe inkjet printers already clock their
  nozzles from an encoder while a hand moves them. Turning that round (the paper is pulled past a
  fixed head by the performer's own withdrawal) removes the motor, the noise and the stall time
  from a thermal or inkjet gimmick. I found no magic product that does this.
- **Thermal printing has natural disguises that magic under-uses**: receipts, boarding passes,
  lottery and parking tickets, shipping labels, event wristbands and name badges are all direct
  thermal. Likewise 12-nozzle thermal inkjet is exactly what prints "best before" and lot codes,
  so a late-printed date code on a sealed product is technologically indistinguishable from a
  factory one.
- **Real ink in the performer's own handwriting is now a hidden-engine output.** Small pen
  plotters (160 x 101 mm travel in a 36 x 23.5 x 11 cm kit; much smaller DIY designs) plus
  few-shot handwriting synthesis (one to five reference samples in 2024 to 2026 papers) give a
  swami gimmick with no thumb. Limit: own handwriting and playing cards only, never third-party
  signatures or documents.
- **A new medium for the nail writer: metal.** Electrochemical etch marking (a wet pad or wick,
  low voltage, a few seconds; figures unverified) darkens steel and nickel alloys permanently,
  and Brother sells $22 thermal stencil tape that turns a label printer into the stencil maker. A thumb-worn etch
  stylus or a thermally printed stencil would put a chosen word on a borrowed key or coin
  (speculative as a magic gimmick; the industrial process is standard).
- **Brute force has a clear crossover point.** Up to 52 outs, a manual index wins (Dex: 52 cards
  in one pocket, 3 to 5 s). From about 50 to 200 physical outs a motorised reel or carousel is
  pocketable. Above about 200 separate objects the volume (roughly 0.3 cm3 per folded billet) pushes
  the index into a table or case, and print-on-demand wins unless the out must be something a
  printer cannot make (real ink, foil, engraving, a sealed commercial object).
- **Information density is no longer the limit.** A 1200 dpi laser printer holds legible text at
  about 1 pt (0.35 mm); a $25 film photoplot holds 10 to 25 micron features, so all 1,000
  three-digit numbers fit in a few square millimetres and all can ride behind a Stanhope-style
  peep lens or a UV exposure mask. A de Bruijn digit sequence lets overlapping windows share
  digits and cuts the travel of a "window that frames one number" by a factor of three.
- **One surface can carry roughly 4 to 6 practical independent messages** (visible, UV
  fluorescent, heat, wet, viewing angle, polariser, breath), and in principle 10 or more; the
  cost is that unused channels stay on the object the spectator keeps.
- **An index can sit in full view if its outs are indistinguishable.** In two-sheet visual
  cryptography, one fixed noise sheet can be paired with a computed "partner" noise sheet for any
  message at all. A hundred partner sheets look identical, so the index needs no hiding, only a
  way to count to the right one.
- **Card re-ordering machines exist but are shoebox-sized.** Open-source camera shufflers
  (about $200, eight bins) and commercial sorters (60 to 100 cards per minute) can stack a
  shuffled deck in one to two minutes; nothing found fits a card case. The casino Deckmate 2
  already reads every card with an internal camera.
- **Overhyped for this slice**: volumetric "prints in seconds" (real, but quote-only lab
  machines), hidden coin presses and embossers (force and noise), cyanotype (10 to 15 minute
  exposures and a wash), Zink (about 50 s per print), hidden FDM printing (minutes and fans).

# Report 20-passive-mechanical-intelligence.md


## Summary (20)
- **There is now a published design vocabulary for "matter that computes", and almost all of it
  is printable or castable at desk scale.** Bistable beams as bits, snap-through as a switching
  event, and coupled bistable cells as gates are demonstrated in FDM ABS at 250 mm scale (Song et
  al. 2019), in single-piece printed cell lattices with a combination lock as the demo (Ion et
  al. 2017), and in cast silicone slabs that count squeezes (Kwakernaak and van Hecke 2023).
- **The single most transferable result is the counting and sequence-recognising slab.** A
  rubber block with slits counts compression cycles up to 10, resets when the squeeze is fully
  released, and a "lock and key" variant reaches one particular internal state only if three
  different squeeze depths arrive in the order B, A, C. A hand-held version works with manual
  squeezing. It is one material, with no separate parts.
- **Delay is the cheapest new primitive.** A viscoelastic dome held inverted and then released
  stays put and snaps back later. The 2025 "metashell" paper programs that delay from seconds to
  2.4 days. The physics (Gomez, Moulton and Vella 2019) says why it is touchy: the delay scales as
  the inverse square root of how close the shell is to true bistability, so small geometry or
  temperature changes move it a lot. Viscous dashpots (a ball sinking through silicone oil) are
  the more predictable alternative: my estimate is 30 s to 5 min for a 3 mm steel ball over 10 mm
  depending on oil grade.
- **Puzzle and lock craft already contains every "condition" a magician could want**, and
  hobbyists have printable examples: open only when spun (centrifugal balls), only after a tilt
  sequence (a 1929 gravity-lock patent and a 1999 "sequential attitude-responsive" puzzle patent),
  only after a tap, only one way up. What magic has not done is treat these as *who-is-holding-it
  discriminators* and as *out selectors* instead of as puzzles.
- **Rate dependence separates performer from spectator.** A dashpot, a seat-belt-style inertia
  pawl or a viscoelastic latch gives an object that yields to slow handling and locks against
  fast handling, or the reverse. "Pull as hard as you can" then guarantees failure, and the prop
  contains nothing a spectator would call a mechanism.
- **Frequency is an addressing scheme.** Printed cantilever reeds follow simple beam formulas;
  my estimate is that a 0.8 mm x 40 mm PLA reed sits near 135 Hz and that 6 to 8 reeds per octave
  can be told apart. One of several identical-looking objects on a shared base can be made to
  respond to a rhythm, a hum or a tuning fork while the others stay still. The pendulum version
  is a standard physics lecture demonstration.
- **Electronics-free fluidic logic is now a desktop print.** A TPU bistable valve prints in about
  3 h on a Prusa MK3S and switches at about 134 kPa; whole grippers with embedded control print
  in one piece; modular printed gates respond in about 100 ms. They need a pressure source, so in
  magic they suit a squeezed bulb, a blown tube or a hidden syringe.
- **Printed plastic is a poor long-term spring.** Printed PLA loses 11 to 14% of its stress in
  the first 500 s at room temperature. Folded PLA, PETG and nylon hinges fail in the low hundreds
  of cycles; TPU and polypropylene survive one to two million. Design rule: never store a printed
  mechanism cocked; make the as-printed shape the armed state, or use TPU, polypropylene or a
  steel spring for the energy store.
- **Classical magic already has mechanical state** (Okito box 1909, Lippincott box about 1949,
  Tenyo's plastic mechanisms, locking card boxes), but always as rigid separate parts with a seam
  somewhere. What is new is: no seam (one printed piece, moving parts hidden behind layer lines);
  state that depends on history (count, order, elapsed time, rate); and unit cost low enough
  (cents to a few dollars) that the prop can be broken open or given away every show.
- **Inverse design exists but stops short of a hobbyist button.** Differentiable design of
  multistable structures from a target energy curve, optimisation of planar multistable
  mechanisms, and a path-to-shape code for rolling "trajectoids" are published. In practice an
  LLM writing parametric CAD plus beam formulas plus two or three print-and-test rounds is the
  realistic loop for single bistables, reeds, centrifugal thresholds and dashpots.

# Report 21-performer-augmentation.md


## Summary (21)
- **The classical body-worn devices are already latch-and-spring machines, and their weaknesses
  are specific and fixable.** A pull or holdout stores energy in elastic and releases it; what it
  lacks is a latch that is not the performer's own arm or knees, a silent stop, a re-arm without
  the hands, and independence from a loose long sleeve. Buyer reviews of current products repeat
  the same four complaints: needs wide sleeves, fails in short sleeves, needs fiddly personal
  adjustment, and (disputed) noise [10][11]. Each of those now has an off-the-shelf engineering
  answer.
- **A sub-millimetre textile electrostatic clutch is the missing latch.** EPFL's textile clutch
  holds about 20 N per cm2 at 300 V, weighs 30 mg per cm2, draws about 1 mW per cm2 and engages or
  releases in under 15 ms [31]; a 2025 Northwestern design holds 22 N on 1 cm2 at 100 V [34]. It
  has no moving parts, so it has no click, and because it is a variable-friction brake it can
  also stop a moving line quietly. This is lab-built today, but the construction is film,
  electrode and a small high-voltage supply.
- **The 150 ms target is a spring problem, not a motor problem.** Moving a half-dollar-sized load
  35 cm in 150 ms needs about 0.17 J and a 4.7 W peak (my calculation). A 10 x 12 mm N20 gearmotor
  tops out near 1.5 W [63], so a motor alone manages roughly 0.3 to 0.4 s. A spring pre-loaded by
  that same motor and released by a silent latch does it easily. The motor's job becomes the
  hands-free reset.
- **Speed alone no longer hides anything from a phone.** Human pursuit fails above roughly
  90 deg/s and a corrective saccade takes 200 ms or more [64], so a 150 ms exit beats the eye. At
  240 fps a phone records 36 frames of it. The design rule is therefore: travel under cover, and
  make the exposed gap a few centimetres, crossed in under two high-speed frames.
- **Garment-scale conveyance exists in the HCI literature and nobody in magic has used it.**
  Calico (UMD, 2022) is an 18 g robot that runs on a sewn-in track at 115 to 227 mm/s carrying
  20 g, with turntables to change route [36]. That is a borrowed ring carried from one cuff to
  the other, or to the collar, in about six seconds with both hands in view. A motor-driven cord
  loop in a bonded fabric channel does the same job faster and more simply.
- **Pneumatics in the lining are now silent and flat.** A Murata piezo microblower is
  20 x 20 x 1.85 mm, runs at 26 kHz (above hearing), and gives about 2.5 kPa and 1.2 L/min [43].
  Heat-sealed pouch motors give 28% stroke and up to 100 N [44]. Together: pockets that open
  themselves, loads that eject themselves, and suction ports that hold a card with no grip. A
  spring-driven bellows and a lay-flat duct could draw a loose banknote or card up a sleeve with
  nothing attached to it (speculative, section 4.1).
- **Extra digits are easy to learn and easy to control with the feet.** The Third Thumb is a
  3D-printed flexible digit pulled by two wrist motors through Bowden cables and steered by
  pressure sensors under the big toes; 98% of 596 members of the public could use it within a
  minute [48][49]. The transferable part is less the thumb than the control scheme: two
  proportional, invisible, learnable channels in the shoes, where magic currently uses on/off
  toe switches [18][19].
- **Most of what implants offer performers is available without surgery.** Implanted magnets and
  NFC chips are in real use by at least three magicians [76][79][80]. A magnet glued to a nail or
  hidden under a ring gives a weaker version of the same sensing [68], NFC tags are sold as nail
  stickers [81][82][83], and one magician's password-locked implant is the cautionary tale [80].
  Risks are stated in section 2.11; no procedure is recommended.
- **An implanted or nail-mounted magnet is a battery-free receiver.** A coil in a watch can
  vibrate a magnet in the hand at 16 to 640 Hz as a private haptic channel [67]. The performer
  carries no electronics on the hand at all.
- **Bare arms remain the hard case.** With short sleeves the surviving real estate is the nail,
  the ring, the watch and its strap, the waistband, the collar and the shoes. The ideas that
  still work there are suction or adhesion at the wrist, magnets and tags on the nail, toe

# Report 22-instrumented-environment.md


## Summary (22)
- **A research-grade sensing mat already has the exact footprint and thickness of a close-up pad.**
  Microsoft's Project Zanzibar mat is 30 x 42 cm and 2.5 mm thick, rolls to a 50 mm tube, reads
  and locates up to 16 NFC tags up to 30 mm above the surface, and images touches and object
  footprints at 7 mm pitch with 2 mm accuracy, on two 400 mAh cells for 2+ hours [1]. Its NFC
  antennas are litz wire zigzag-stitched onto stretch cotton with a consumer sewing machine [1].
  Nothing sold to magicians comes close to this.
- **What magic dealers sell today is single-function and thick.** The Faraday Pad needs a 30 mm
  wooden frame plus a 14 mm, 0.84 kg black box underneath [4]; the Quantum Pad is 74 mm thick and
  3.5 kg with a 73 x 98 mm working area [3]; scales are separate 20 mm bricks or card boxes
  [7][8]; RFID readers are separate 77 x 33 mm units read at 11 cm [11]. None fuses modalities,
  none identifies unprepared objects by footprint, and none lives in the chairs, floor or lighting.
- **Sensing fits in a pad; actuation mostly does not.** Antennas, capacitive matrices, Hall grids
  (2 mm [25]), heater films, 0.5 mm power coils [45] and piezo pickups fit in 3 to 6 mm. Gantries,
  load cells, electropermanent magnets, air plenums, cameras and coil arrays with useful force
  need 15 to 80 mm and belong in a tray, tabletop, apron or the performer's case.
- **The strongest use of the surface is to know and to prepare, not to visibly act.** When an
  object moves by itself on a mat, the mat is the only causal candidate. When the mat merely knows
  (which object left, where, in what order, what it weighs) the effect happens in the performer's
  mouth or the spectator's hands and the surface is never in the frame.
- **Furniture can weigh people, which is far more reliable than reading their pulse.** A chair
  accelerometer identifies who is sitting (96% balanced accuracy over 105 people [31]), but
  heart-rate changes detect concealed knowledge only weakly (d about 0.89, AUC about 0.74 in lab
  protocols with repeated questions [34]). A stool on load cells that notices a 250 g object
  arriving is a much better "who has it" method than any physiology.
- **Lighting is an unexamined actor.** A multi-channel LED source can change the colour of chosen
  dyes while still looking white (NTT, shown at Paris Fashion Week 2024 [39]); modulation above
  roughly 2 to 3 kHz is invisible even during eye movements [40], so a lamp can broadcast timing
  or data to anything with a photodiode.
- **Slow and unattended methods are nearly free.** 96.6% of 417 viewers missed a colour change
  that unfolded over 16 s in the centre of a video [41]. Shelf-scale props can move millimetres
  per minute, or only when sensors say nobody is facing them.
- **The glass table is an opportunity, not a constraint.** It "proves" there is nothing in the
  surface while exposing the face of every face-down card to a camera at floor level.
- **Borrowed objects increasingly arrive pre-tagged.** Bank cards, transit cards, hotel keys and
  phones answer a 13.56 MHz reader through a wallet at 30 mm [1]. A mat can fingerprint a wallet
  by the set of tags inside it without reading any payment data (legal and ethical limits in 2.2).
- **Trust has a history.** Robert-Houdin replaced floor-length draped tables with light undraped
  ones [50] and hid his electromagnet under the floor [51]. The modern equivalent: keep the pad
  thin, rollable and handed out, and put the method in something nobody thinks of as a prop.

# Report 24-services-and-public-infrastructure.md


## Summary (24)
- **The outside world is now an API with a one-unit minimum.** A postcard costs $0.91 by API
  (Lob), a phone number $1.15 a month (Twilio), an 8-second play on a roadside billboard cents
  (Blip), a one-off printed book $2.30 to print (KDP), a one-off deck $16.95 (MPC), a courier
  $8 to $10 (Uber Direct, DoorDash Drive), a tasked 50 cm satellite photograph about $200 to
  $300 (SkyFi). The television-budget "prediction out in the world" is a hobby-budget item.
- **The binding constraint is lead time and approval, not money.** Almost nothing physical can be
  made to exist in under an hour. Mail takes 5 to 7 business days, print-on-demand 4 to 9 days,
  billboard creative 1 to 3 business days to approve. Only telephony, messaging, pre-approved
  screens, couriers and (probably) same-day photo counters respond inside a performance.
- **So the natural architecture is "stage many, select live".** Pay for N genuine pre-placed outs
  days ahead (envelopes, lockers, caches, approved creatives, phone numbers) and let the live
  choice pick one. Out capacity per channel is in the table in 2.8.
- **Live-triggered channels suit transposition and transmission plots, not prediction plots.**
  Anything ordered after the choice carries an order time on a receipt, label or log. Frame it as
  "it is appearing over there now", never as "it was always there".
- **Honest timestamps prove "existed before", never "the only one".** OpenTimestamps, Wayback
  captures, notarisation, copyright registration and domain creation dates are all genuine and
  cheap or free, and all are compatible with making fifty-two of them. The strongest commitment
  is therefore one the spectator has physically held (posted mail), because siblings cannot hide
  in their hands. The cleverest structure found: distribute the siblings across the audience.
- **Third-party fulfilment is a "prove ordinariness" machine.** A gimmicked book or stacked,
  marked deck that the spectator bought themselves from Amazon or a card printer's shop, and
  that the performer never touches, is a new method architecture. This is the single most
  transferable finding.
- **Public telemetry turns "chance" into knowledge.** Aircraft overhead (OpenSky, free, 5 to 10 s
  resolution), the fleet number and licence plate of the next bus (GTFS-realtime), ISS passes ten
  days ahead, and above all stream latency (a "live" stream is 15 to 78 s in the past) give the
  performer facts about the public world minutes or seconds before the audience.
- **Real-time voice agents make every owned phone number a collaborator** for $0.07 to $0.30 a
  minute with sub-second response; inbound calls only (outbound automated calls need consent).
- **Independent strangers are the best witnesses.** A geocache with fourteen months of dated
  finder logs, or a parcel with carrier tracking from another city (alternate-reality-game
  craft), is evidence no prop can supply. The secret work happens the day before, off-stage.
- **Clean lines:** do not back-date listings, plant false notices, edit reference sites, disguise
  a trick as a commercial advert to a sign owner, call people with an automated voice without
  consent, or mine guests' private data. Every concept below runs on the performer's own
  accounts, openly supplied guest information, and paid or willing open collaborators.

# Report 25-thinness-concealment-duplication.md


## Summary (25)
- **The useful unit of thinness in card magic is "one card" = about 0.30 mm and 1.8 g (about
  320 g/m2).** A split card half is about 0.12 to 0.17 mm. Several functional materials now fit
  inside that budget with room to spare and can be ordered by a hobbyist: laser-cut 304 stainless
  at 0.03 to 0.10 mm from PCB-stencil houses (from about $3 a sheet), photo-etched metals down to
  0.01 mm, polyester film at 1.4 to 12 microns, and 0.2 mm resin-printed walls.
- **PCB solder-paste stencils are an overlooked precision sheet-metal service.** JLCPCB cuts 304
  stainless from 0.03 to 0.5 mm with apertures down to about 0.08 mm, in any outline, for a few
  dollars. That is a spring, hinge, shim, mask and inlay factory for flap cards and thin gimmicks.
- **A "double card" (0.6 mm) is now a viable electronics chassis**: catalogue lithium-polymer cells
  exist at 0.4 mm (7.5 to 30 mAh), which leaves 0.1 mm skins on either side. Pointer to agent 14.
- **The coin shell remains a craft secret**: I found no published wall thickness or fit tolerance
  for an expanded shell, only makers' statements that each coin in a set is trued to identical size
  and the shell expanded "only enough". The modern route is not to beat the turners at coins but to
  apply the shell principle to any scanned object (0.02 to 0.05 mm scanners, 0.2 mm resin walls,
  0.1 mm electroformed copper).
- **Concealment-by-angle is available as a 0.5 mm film**: micro-louvre light-control film passes
  light within a 60 degree cone and is black outside it. Nothing in the magic literature I searched
  uses it as a one-viewer window.
- **Tearing is more reproducible than magicians assume, crumpling is less.** Fracture-mechanics
  work treats tear paths in thin sheets as reproducible and steerable by pre-cuts and perforations,
  while crumple networks are not reproducible in detail (only total crease length is). So a
  "programmed tear" is plausible and a "programmed crumple" is not.
- **Fastest credible duplication pipelines**: a printed flat item, about 2 to 4 minutes (scan,
  dye-sub print at 23 to 59 s, cut). A small monochrome solid, about 20 to 40 minutes (silicone
  putty mould cures in 7 minutes, urethane demoulds in 10). Anything needing a scan, print and
  paint is an overnight job, not an interval job.
- **Signature duplication is an established theatrical technique with a deep literature** (the
  Conjuring Archive lists 123 "Signed Duplicate" entries from 1905 on) and current TV practice is a
  person tracing over a lightbox onto a split card layer. A camera plus a pen plotter holding the
  same marker is the obvious unbuilt upgrade.
- **What exposes a duplicate depends entirely on who is looking.** Collectors use a loupe (offset
  rosette versus inkjet dither), weight to 0.03 g, light transmission and feel. Lay audiences apply
  none of these; they check gist, their own mark, and (if they handle it) feel. Match stock and
  finish first, print second.
- **Legal lines are narrow and clear**: no colour reproductions of US currency unless one-sided and
  under 75% or over 150% size with files destroyed; imitation coins must be marked "COPY"; never
  reproduce signatures on anything but theatrical props.

# Report 27-speed-and-compaction.md


## Summary (27)
- **Speed is cheap; the latch, the stop and the timing are what cost.** A 6 g coin needs only 10 g
  of acceleration (0.6 N, 0.3 J) to cover 500 mm in about 100 ms, which is inside the 90 to 150 ms
  it takes the eye to start pursuit. Classical elastic already delivers this. What engineering adds
  is a release with no pre-motion, a stop with no click, and a trigger timed better than a person
  can time it.
- **Contrast, not velocity, sets the speed requirement.** Vision integrates over roughly 100 to
  120 ms. A moving object smears into a streak whose contrast is the object's contrast times
  (time it dwells at a point / integration time). A bright coin on black must clear its own width
  in about 3 ms (8 m/s) to streak below threshold; the same coin within 10% of the background
  luminance needs under 1 m/s. Background matching buys a tenfold cut in required speed (derived).
- **There are three "instant" thresholds, and they differ for eyes and cameras.** About 100 ms
  (cannot be tracked), about 30 to 40 ms (no intermediate state seen; one frame of ordinary video),
  and about 4 ms (one frame of 240 fps phone slow motion). Mechanical props can reach the first two.
  Only electro-optic changes, shutter blades and elastic membrane retraction reach the third.
- **Latch-mediated spring actuation (LaMSA) is the right design vocabulary for fast props.** The
  biology and robotics literature since 2018 treats motor, spring and latch as one coupled system.
  Hobby-scale results: a 30 g device going 0 to 28 m/s in 9 ms; a 1.5 g striker reaching 26 m/s;
  a toy popper snapping in under 5 ms. The latch geometry, not the spring, decides how clean the
  release is.
- **Audiences blink together, and they do it just after an effect lands.** Wiseman and Nakano
  (2016) found blink onsets synchronised within plus or minus 150 ms across viewers, with
  synchronised blinks right after each climax and on 4 of 7 secret actions. VR researchers have
  since built systems that detect one person's saccades and blinks and change the scene during
  them. Nobody appears to have pointed that at a physical prop.
- **Deployable-structure engineering beats every classical compaction benchmark on paper.** The
  appearing cane is about 17:1 in length. A coilable lattice mast stows in under 1% of its length
  (100:1) at under 35 g per metre and deploys on its own strain energy. Bistable reeled composite
  tubes (RolaTube and NASA booms) give a load-bearing pole from a hand-sized coil. Rigid-chain
  actuators give 10:1 at 1 m/s.
- **Collapse on a trigger now has several non-classical routes.** Layer jamming (a stack of
  sheets held rigid by vacuum, limp when air is let in), multistable inflatable origami (rigid
  without pressure, snaps flat), drop-stitch panels (board-stiff at about 1 bar, cloth when
  vented) and vacuum-driven origami (90% contraction).
- **Thinner sheet is the least glamorous and largest compaction gain.** Magicians' silk is about
  22 g/m2. Ultralight 7-denier nylon is 19 g/m2 at $18.50 per yard. Indoor-model film is
  1.4 g/m2: sixteen times the area of silk in the same packed volume. Four square metres of
  metallised film is about 4 cm3 of solid material.
- **Slow things to rule out early:** shape-memory alloy, electrochromics, e-paper (120 to 250 ms
  and it flashes), memory foam (hours to recover from tight packing), mechanical flip-dots (60 to
  70 ms and a loud click).
- **Safety scales with speed squared.** Anything above 10 m/s near a face, spring-steel edges,
  CO2 cartridges (frost, noise), capacitor-driven launchers (high voltage) and nitrocellulose need
  explicit hazard controls. Several of the best ideas below avoid all of these by moving nothing
  further than a fraction of a millimetre.
