# Task 21: performer augmentation

Report path: /home/user/verified-gifs-only/magic-research/21-performer-augmentation.md

Your slice: **augmenting the performer: wearable robotics, soft actuators in clothing, body-worn conveyance and concealment, and body modification, as the modern descendants of the sleeve holdout, the pull, the topit and the thumb tip.** Sleight of hand is limited by ten fingers, two sleeves and human speed. Ask what wearable engineering changes about secretly holding, moving, delivering, stealing and ditching objects on the body, and about what the performer can sense.

Lines of inquiry (find more):

- The classical baseline, with its limits stated as engineering specs: sleeve holdouts (Kepplinger, Martin, Sullivan types and modern makers), elastic and reel pulls, the Jack Miller holdout, topits and pocket systems, body loads, servantes, thumb tips and finger shells, wrist and arm reels (ITR, Tarantula-type motorised reels), magnetic holders under jackets, the Raven and sleeving techniques, quick-change costume engineering. What do performers say fails (noise, bulk, arm motion needed, reset, range of travel, speed)?
- Wearable soft robotics and exosuit technology at garment scale: cable-driven soft exosuits (Harvard Walsh lab) and their Bowden-cable actuators, twisted-string actuators (very compact, quiet, high force: numbers), SMA-wire and SMA-spring garments, pneumatic pouch motors and thin McKibben muscles, fabric-based pneumatic actuators, electrostatic clutches in textiles (Hinchet, Shea: sub-millimetre clutches holding 100+ N), HASEL and DEA in clothing, knitted and woven actuators ("active textiles", OmniFiber), quiet micro-pumps, tendon routing through clothing, motorised micro-winches, tape and zipper actuators, constant-force springs from badge reels and tape measures, magnetic guidance tracks in garments, and garment-integrated conveyance: robots that crawl on or inside clothing (Rovables, MIT; "Calico" on-garment rail robots, UMD 2022), rail or conveyor systems that carry a small object from wrist to pocket or collar invisibly, peristaltic sleeves. For each: force, stroke, speed (can it take a card or coin from palm to elbow in under 150 ms, quieter than 30 dB?), bulk, power.
- Supernumerary and augmenting limbs: the Third Thumb (Dani Clode; UCL studies on rapid learning), supernumerary robotic fingers (MIT d'Arbeloff lab, Prattichizzo's Sixth Finger), jamming and soft grippers at glove scale, exo-gloves and tendon gloves (silent finger assistance for sleights: holding a palm with no muscular tension), fingertip micro-grippers, retractable fingernail or ring tools, electroadhesive and gecko fingertip pads (holding a card or coin on an open flat palm facing down with no grip: a "palm" that needs no palm), micro suction cups and octopus-inspired adhesive patches for skin, skin-safe temporary adhesives, magnet-based holds on the hand (ring magnets, magnetic gel nails, implanted magnets: see below), flesh-coloured carriers made by modern prosthetics (pointer; agent 15).
- Fast release and launch from the body: latch-mediated spring actuation literature (LaMSA: mantis shrimp, trap-jaw ant; engineered versions) and what speed makes a small object's exit invisible (leaves the visual field within one or two video frames, or inside the window where the eye fails to track); compact launchers and retractors (a coin or card that leaves the fingertips for the sleeve or collar faster than a saccade), vacuum retraction, elastic energy storage density, silent stops and catchers (how to decelerate without a click), and safety near faces. Agent 27 covers speed mechanisms in general; focus on body-worn versions.
- Sensing and feedback on the performer's body: smart-ring and EMG wristband input (pointer; agent 10), haptic sleeves and belts as rich private displays, tongue and tooth interfaces (pointer; agent 15), thin skin-mounted haptics, eyes-free displays in the collar or cuff; sensory substitution and augmentation (magnetic sense via fingertip magnets or the North Sense / feelSpace belt: a performer who can physically feel a hidden magnet or a live wire; ultrasonic or thermal sense; hearing aids as hidden superhuman hearing with directional beamforming).
- Body modification and biohacking used by performers: implanted NFC/RFID chips (Dangerous Things xNT, xSIID LED implants; the magician community around them such as Anastasia Synn; what a phone or reader can do with an implant), fingertip magnet implants (lifting force, sensing sensitivity, risks), sub-dermal LEDs, magnetic tattoos, and long-wear temporary alternatives that avoid surgery (nail-mounted or under-ring tags and magnets). Give medical risks plainly and do not recommend procedures; report the capability and the non-invasive substitutes.
- Clothing as apparatus: hidden channels and pockets enabled by bonded seams and 3D knitting, garments with embedded wiring and coils (inductive power or data to a prop held in the hand; a sleeve that reads a card), magnet-lined or ferrous-lined zones, heating/cooling zones to manage a thermally triggered gimmick, shoes as actuator and sensor housings (toe switches, heel power packs, weight sensing), and hair, hats, glasses, watches, jewellery and face masks as housings.
- Training technology: motion capture, high-speed video and ML feedback for practising sleights, tools that compute angles and flashes automatically (what an audience member at each seat can see: simple 3D visibility simulation), haptic coaching. This changes how fast a hobbyist can acquire classical skill, which combines with the technology.

For each capability: numbers, hardware and cost, concealment under ordinary clothing (short sleeves are the gold standard: say what still works with bare arms), noise, reset, and safety. In "Effect concepts" propose 8 to 12 mechanisms; favour ones that keep the spectator-handled objects ordinary and put all technology on the performer.

Neighbouring slices handled by others (do not go deep): prosthetics, dental and textile trades (agent 15); smart-material actuator fundamentals (agent 7); covert sensing and cueing channels (agent 10); haptics aimed at spectators (agent 11); instrumented tables and rooms (agent 22); speed and compaction mechanisms in general (agent 27).
