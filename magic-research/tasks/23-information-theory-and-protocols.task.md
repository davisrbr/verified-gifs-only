# Task 23: information theory, coding, cryptography and protocols as method structure

Your slice: **results from information theory, coding theory, cryptography, combinatorics and theoretical computer science that could become the hidden structure of a method.** No hardware is required for most of this; the "technology" is a protocol. Magic already uses a little of it (the Fitch Cheney five-card trick, de Bruijn sequences, Gilbreath). The academic literature has moved far beyond what magicians use. Ask what each result lets a performer do: transmit, hide, commit, identify, or prove something under conditions that look impossible.

Lines of inquiry (find more):

- Card-based cryptography and physical protocols: the "five-card trick" of den Boer (1989) for secure AND, and the large literature since (Mizuki, Shizuya, Koch, Nishimura, Abe and others): secure computation with a deck of cards, committed-format protocols, private permutations, pile-scramble shuffles, protocols using polarising plates, envelopes, PEZ dispensers, coins, balls and bags, and marbles; physical zero-knowledge proofs for puzzles (Sudoku ZK by Gradwohl, Naor, Pinkas & Rothblum; ZK for Kakuro, Slitherlink, Nonograms, Rubik's cube and so on, many by Robert, Miyahara, Ruangwises, Bultel et al.); "Where's Waldo" ZK (Naor, Naor & Reingold); the dating problem / socialist millionaires with cards. These are procedures in which a group handles real cards and something is provably computed or proved while inputs stay hidden. Which make good magic as they stand (the honest version is already astonishing), and which can be subverted by a performer who secretly breaks one assumption (a marked card, a false shuffle) so that information that "provably cannot leak" does?
- Covert channels with honest-looking actions: how many bits can be sent through the order of cards (52! is about 225 bits; a small packet's order as a message: permutation coding, ranking and unranking), the orientation of cards or coins, which of several equivalent actions is chosen, timing, word choice in natural patter (linguistic steganography, including LLM-based steganography with arithmetic coding where an innocent sentence carries a hidden payload), pauses, where an object is put down. Classical two-person telepathy codes (the Zancigs, the Piddingtons, verbal and silent codes) restated as channel coding: capacity, error rates, and how modern coding (error-correcting codes, arithmetic coding against a language model) could make a spoken code undetectable and high-rate. Rolling shared secrets (TOTP-style) so that the same innocent phrase means different things each minute and cannot be decoded by a spectator who knows the system. Precise shared time as a hidden shared random number.
- Coding-theory tricks and their generalisations: Hamming-code "find the lie" card sets and the Ulam liar game (identify one of N with q questions when the volunteer may lie once or twice: the audience is told they may lie, and the performer still gets it); the Cheney trick generalised (hide 1 of n cards; optimal n = k! + k - 1; variants where the volunteer chooses which card to hide; 3-card and 4-card versions; versions with the deck face down); hat-guessing protocols and the 100-prisoners results as two-performer or performer-plus-audience routines; the "impossible chessboard" / coin-flip puzzle (one coin flipped on a board encodes a location: XOR/Hamming structure) as a stage piece; combinatorial group testing (find the one chosen item among N with log N pooled tests: for example which of 64 glasses, envelopes or people, with tests that look like innocent sorting); compressed sensing and non-adaptive search; superimposed codes; Gray codes and universal cycles for objects other than cards (permutations, subsets, words: a stack of 120 tiles where any 3 consecutive identify the position); de Bruijn tori and position-coding patterns (Anoto dot paper and its successors: any tiny window of a printed surface uniquely identifies where it is: applied to card backs, a tablecloth, wallpaper, a jigsaw, so that a small camera glimpse or a torn corner identifies everything); perfect maps; locating arrays.
- Cryptography as theatre and as method: commitment schemes (a prediction "locked" by a published hash, a timestamped blockchain entry, a sealed envelope) and the ways a commitment can be made equivocal for theatre (chameleon hashes, trapdoor commitments, multiple pre-registered commitments, hash of a statement with a free variable); secret sharing (Shamir; visual cryptography of Naor & Shamir: random-looking transparencies that reveal an image when stacked, and extended visual cryptography where each share is itself a meaningful picture; colour and multi-secret schemes; rotation-based schemes where one pair of sheets reveals different images at different rotations: printable today, largely unused in magic); physically unclonable functions and unclonable tokens as "proof this is the same object"; oblivious transfer and private information retrieval as plots (the performer learns which book you took without learning anything else, apparently); verifiable shuffles and mental poker (a shuffle that everyone can verify is fair, yet...); zero-knowledge "I know your card and can prove it without naming it"; time-lock puzzles; pseudo-randomness that passes every audience test.
- Algorithms and combinatorics with a magical reading: perfect hashing and minimal questioning for book tests; minimal progressive and branching anagrams by search; error-tolerant fishing; sorting networks and "the audience sorts it without knowing" procedures; parity and invariant arguments (which make self-working tricks robust to free choices); Latin and magic squares, orthogonal arrays and Steiner systems (the Kirkman/Fano structure behind "dobble/Spot It" cards: any two cards share exactly one symbol) as forcing structures; graph and matching theorems (Hall's theorem card tricks; "any five cards contain..." pigeonhole results; Erdős–Szekeres); Kruskal-count-like coupling arguments; shuffling theory invariants; the mathematics of the "Australian" and other elimination deals; self-synchronising codes so that a spectator can start anywhere; probability paradoxes that make a sure thing look like a bet (non-transitive dice: Efron, Grime dice, non-transitive coins/Penney's game) and newer results (2016 to 2026) in recreational mathematics journals, the Gathering 4 Gardner exchange books, the College Mathematics Journal, Math Horizons, Journal of Recreational Mathematics successors, arXiv math.HO/math.CO/cs.CR with "card trick" or "magic trick" in the abstract. Collect at least 20 specific recent papers with one line each on the trick they imply.
- What computation adds: every scheme above was once limited by what a person can compute or memorise on stage. With a hidden computer and a cue channel, or with a pre-computed lookup printed invisibly, decoding cost is zero. Which previously impractical schemes become practical (large-n Cheney, long liar games, group testing with noisy answers, real-time arithmetic-coded patter)?
